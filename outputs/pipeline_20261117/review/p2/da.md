## Review Body

**Calibration status.** `NOT_CALIBRATED`

**Why D3 is a warn and not a block.** The thesis as the manuscript mostly words it is descriptive: two specific checkpoints differed in closed-loop plan rates at low speed, the difference showed in standstill time only with the controller's gate, and the checkpoint with fewer stay-put plans did not score better. That descriptive thesis stands on the reported numbers, and the arithmetic I could check is consistent (the four standstill bands sum to the totals in Table S2, the blocked and non-blocked parts sum to the H2 difference, the two-pass means match the single-pass values). What fails is a set of links that go beyond the description: one sentence that attributes the change to data composition, the reading of blocked time as no extra cost, the reading of the history-swap test as showing no dependence, the weight placed on the third checkpoint, and the step from closed-loop rates to a difference at model level. Each is a link or a secondary claim that is overstated or rests on evidence that cannot discriminate. None is contradicted outright by the manuscript's own data, and each can be repaired by rewording or by an analysis the existing logs or infrastructure allow. The five are listed in the MAJOR table and argued below.

**What the manuscript does well.** It discloses when each rule was written relative to its data, including the looks at incomplete data. It runs a closed-loop ablation without the gate instead of relying on offline accounting. It reports results that cut against its own framing (lower Driving Score for the balanced checkpoint, no difference at 1,600 samples, the second pass failing the H2 rule).

**Strongest counter-argument.** A scholar holding the opposite view would say that the manuscript documents one unusual training run and a controller artefact, and shows nothing about data composition. The study contains two tests of the same composition contrast. At 1,600 samples the low-speed stay-put shares of the two training sets differ by nineteen points and the two checkpoints do not differ (8.86 % against 9.54 %). At 6,400 samples one checkpoint, Golden-6400, departs from all five others of the original design, including the unadapted policy and its own smaller sibling. The simplest account of six checkpoints with one outlier is that the outlier is a property of that run, or of what 8,000 optimizer steps do to this expert. The third checkpoint does not break that account, because it shares the seed, the schedule, the pool and an unreported fraction of its samples with the outlier. Nor is it shown that the outlier differs in what it plans once slow. Every rate is measured in states each policy put itself into, a policy that stays put produces more at-rest steps in the same scene, and the obstacle-heavy checkpoint plans to brake to rest from speed two to three times as often, so the two policies may be at rest in different places. The standstill effect, the first to be specified in advance of its data, is not met when both arms run on one harness and changes sign without the gate. The checkpoint with fewer stay-put plans then spends about 8 to 14 % of its steps commanded to accelerate at the site of a recorded collision, scores no better in any pass, and by the interval scores worse on the two-pass mean. Read without the authors' framing, the obstacle-heavy checkpoint behaves much like the pretrained model, the balanced checkpoint lost a stopping behaviour that was holding it back at obstacles, and nobody can yet say whether the data mix had anything to do with it.

**M1. Composition is credited in the contribution statement and withdrawn in the discussion.** Contribution (i) says the study measures how two data compositions changed the behaviour. Section 5 says the effect of the mix cannot be separated from that of the training run. Both cannot be the claim. The manuscript's own evidence favours the weaker one. The 1,600-sample pair is a second instance of the same contrast, with nearly the same conditional shares in the training sets (30.6 % against 11.4 %), and it shows no difference at plan level. The authors' conjecture does not predict that null, as they say. So the composition contrast was observed twice and produced a difference once. Minimum remedy: word the title, contribution (i) and the first sentence of the conclusion as a comparison of checkpoints, and state the 1,600-sample null next to the 6,400-sample difference wherever the latter is given. Stronger and costlier: a second training seed for the two 6,400-sample cells, which the authors already name as future work.

**M2. The third checkpoint cannot yet carry the weight placed on it.** The decoupling arm is the manuscript's only answer to the single-run objection (its statement that Golden-6400 is not alone). Three things needed to weigh it are missing or unfavourable. First, the manuscript does not report the low-speed stay-put share of the decoupling set, which is the quantity its own conjecture says matters, so the reader cannot tell whether this checkpoint agrees or disagrees with the conjecture. Second, it does not report how many samples the decoupling set shares with the balanced 6,400 set. The balanced and obstacle-heavy sets already share 67 %, and the decoupling set copies the manoeuvre and stationary targets of the balanced set, so the overlap may be large. Together with the same seed, schedule and step count, that makes the two low-rate checkpoints correlated runs, and their agreement weak evidence against a run-specific outcome. Third, the design has three of four cells: no checkpoint has the balanced scenario-group mix with the obstacle-heavy manoeuvre mix and stationary share, so "not with the scenario-group mix" holds only at the balanced manoeuvre level. The arm also has one evaluation pass set against two-pass means, and its stay-put interval against the balanced checkpoint excludes zero. Minimum remedy: add the decoupling set to Table 1 with its realised shares, report its sample overlap with both other 6,400 sets, and restrict the wording to what three cells allow.

**M3. Blocked time is declared not to be an additional cost on evidence that cannot show that.** The argument is: blocked runs lie at recorded collision positions, collision events are already counted, collision counts do not differ detectably, therefore blocked time is the same low-speed difference seen at a collision site. Three steps do not hold. (a) Collision events are merged by actor, first by the leaderboard and again by the authors, so the count is blind by construction to how long or how often the vehicle pushes against the same object. Twenty-two blocked runs on six routes against two runs on one route is exactly the difference the count cannot register. (b) The sentence that the models differ in "what they command there" presupposes that the obstacle-heavy checkpoint is also at a collision site on those routes. The manuscript reports that it stops and holds on those six routes, and does not report whether it collided there. If it did not, the balanced checkpoint has contacts at those sites that the other avoided, which is an additional cost. (c) The order of contact and blocked run is unknown, as the authors state. The manuscript also says it makes no safety claim in either direction, and "not as an additional harm" (Section 4.10) is a claim in one direction. Minimum remedy: report collision events of both checkpoints on the routes with blocked time, per pass, and replace the "not a cost in addition" reading with a statement of what is and is not known.

**M4. The history-swap test does not discriminate between the accounts it is used to choose between.** The abstract says the test "showed no dependence"; the body says no dependence was detected on eight routes, with intervals that include increases of up to 14 points, on inputs the model was never trained on. Beyond power, the stated prediction is not derivable. The replaced history is a constant 4.0 m/s, which is a cruising history, and the outcome counts a plan that keeps cruising at 4 m/s as "holds speed". An expert that reads the history and continues the motion it reports, which is the copycat form of the inertia account, would return a cruising plan and so keep or raise the share. An expert that ignores the history would keep the share. The observed result (shares up by about five points in both models) is what both predict. The second outcome points the other way from the authors' reading: in the situations where the model stays put, a model told it is moving essentially never planned to slow down (no plan ended at rest, decelerating plans 2.03 % and 0.46 %). If the stay-put plans followed the scene, which is the alternative the section sets against inertia, a scene that justified waiting should have produced braking. A plan with no acceleration, issued whatever the scene, from a state at rest, is behaviourally what the introduction calls a vehicle at rest tending to stay at rest. Minimum remedy: change the abstract and conclusion to "did not detect", state that the primary outcome cannot separate a history-ignoring from a history-continuing expert, and drop the non-attribution to inertia or reduce it to "undetermined".

**M5. A model-level difference is inferred from rates that each model generates for itself.** Section 4.6 states that no share compares the experts on identical inputs and that none measures propensity. Section 5 then calls the result a model-level difference, and Section 4.3 says the model-side argument rests on the gate-off and speed-conditioned shares. Neither removes the dependence on self-generated states. A stay-put plan keeps the vehicle at rest in the same scene, so per-step shares weight a stop by its length, and a small difference per decision becomes a large difference per step. The checkpoints also come to rest for different reasons: the obstacle-heavy one plans to brake to rest from motion two to three times as often, the balanced one is at rest in blocked runs in about a tenth of its at-rest steps. "They are at rest in different places" fits the at-rest shares as well as "they plan differently in the same place". The comparison that would decide it is available: the swap-test agent already logs plans that are not acted on, so each expert could be queried on the other's logged states, or logged low-speed inputs could be replayed offline with many noise draws. Minimum remedy: run that same-input comparison on a subset of routes, or keep the model-level wording out of the headings and conclusions.

**Lesser points (below the Major band).**
- H1 rests on single passes while the manuscript's own noise figure is as large as the effect: the 44-route Driving Score of one checkpoint "moved by 7.40 points" between passes (text: S5 item 4), against an H1 effect of +7.28. From the reported two-pass mean (46.15) and first pass (49.85), the second-pass score of Golden-6400 is about 42.45, level with the unadapted re-run (42.60); the manuscript does not state this value. The abstract gives the p-value without the interval that includes zero. Confidence 4/5 (arithmetic on reported values).
- H2 is reported as met, but the only comparison free of the harness change is the one that does not meet the rule (table: Table S3, row for the second pass alone). The abstract states both; the hypothesis list and Section 4.3 lead with "met". Confidence 4/5.
- The endpoint moved from Driving Score to standstill to plan length, each time after the previous one had been seen on the same routes and checkpoints: standstill "became the H2 endpoint after the Driving Scores of the first 6,400-sample models were seen to be similar" (text: §4.1). H3, called the main finding, re-tests on the same two checkpoints an effect already seen at p near 10^-9, so it could hardly fail and says nothing about composition. The disclosure is complete; the label "main finding" and the phrase "a pre-specified test was met" in the abstract lend it more weight than a re-run carries. Confidence 4/5.
- The title's direction is not what the data show. The unadapted policy has the highest stay-put share ("the stay-put share at low speed was 33.3 % for" Base; text: §4.6), and in Table S2 its standstill with plans under 0.64 m (8.29 % in the re-run) equals that of the obstacle-heavy checkpoint (7.93 %). No checkpoint learned to wait; two checkpoints stopped waiting. Confidence 3/5 (the Base figure is pooled and from the run with the older termination rule).
- The six-checkpoint comparison uses shares pooled over steps (24.9 % for the obstacle-heavy 6,400 checkpoint), while the headline uses means of per-route shares (14.00 % for the same checkpoint and pass; table: Table 4, stay-put row). Pooled shares are dominated by a few long stalls, which the manuscript shows are not reproducible route by route. The statement that Golden-6400 is "the low outlier among the six" should be given on the per-route scale with intervals. Confidence 3/5.

**Ignored or under-weighted alternative explanations.**
1. Where the vehicle stops, not what it plans once stopped (see M5).
2. A run-specific or training-length-specific outcome of the balanced 6,400 run (see M1 and M2).
3. Fine-tuning erodes a stopping behaviour of the pretrained policy at a rate that depends on run length, and the mix plays a minor part: on the pooled low-speed stay-put share the other four fine-tuned checkpoints of the original design all sit between the unadapted policy and Golden-6400.
4. For blocked time: the obstacle-heavy checkpoint's stay-put plans prevent contacts that the balanced checkpoint makes (see M3).

**Missing stakeholder perspectives.** Practitioners who must choose a controller for a given checkpoint; maintainers of the benchmark whose collision merging hides sustained contact; other road users (named by the authors in limitation 11).

**Unexamined premise.** The whole plan-level analysis treats a logged plan with extent under 0.64 m as a decision to wait. Section 3.1 states that "with every control at its training mean, a plan from rest has" an extent of 0.59 m. The stay-put class at rest therefore contains the output of an expert that emits nothing in particular. A stay-put plan may be a default output and not a sampled waiting mode, which would change what "sampling stay-put futures at the rate of the data" means. The authors come close to this in Section 5 ("a difference in how often the model issues a start") without drawing the consequence for the conjecture.

Conclusions follow from their evidence: PARTLY_MEETS (M3, M4). Rival explanations addressed: PARTLY_MEETS (named in most cases, tested in few; M2, M5). Scope within the conditions examined: MEETS for the abstract and conclusion, PARTLY_MEETS for the title and contribution list. Contrary evidence reported and weighed on equal terms: MEETS. Limitations carried into the wording of conclusions: PARTLY_MEETS. All are decision-bearing for D3 only.

**Observations (non-defects).** If the composition claim were dropped entirely, what remains is still usable: the demonstration that total standstill is a joint property of model and controller, and that it sums two components moving in opposite directions. That part does not depend on training seeds. The paper might be stronger if it led with it.
