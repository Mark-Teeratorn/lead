Distribution Curation and Closed-Loop Standstill in Continuous-Action Autonomous Driving: An Empirical Evaluation Across Scale


Abstract

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that couple a high-level reasoning backbone with a continuous Flow-Matching action expert avoid the latency of autoregressive action tokens and the mode averaging of deterministic waypoint regression. However, when fine-tuning a continuous action expert via imitation learning on urban driving logs, naive oversampling of edge-case recordings—a regime we term hazard flooding—inflates the fraction of stationary waiting frames and asymmetric obstacle-avoidance maneuvers. Because the action expert conditions on recent ego-vehicle kinematics, an elevated stationary prior pulls the learned longitudinal acceleration vector field toward zero when ego speed drops near zero, inducing closed-loop standstill paralysis via the inertia problem and causal confusion. We investigate how training data curation and dataset scale interact across a $2 \times 2$ factorial evaluation design ($1,600$ vs. $6,400$ frames; distribution-curated `Golden` vs. hazard-flooded `Flooded`) when fine-tuning the $2.28\text{B}$-parameter Flow-Matching action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 (with its $8\text{B}$ Cosmos-Reason2 VLM backbone frozen in ParoQuant W4A8) on the closed-loop CARLA Bench2Drive benchmark ($N = 44$ canonical routes with $20\text{ Hz}$ telemetry, plus a $219$-route out-of-sample holdout check). At $1,600$ frames, curated fine-tuning (`Golden-1600`) improves composite Driving Score over the unadapted `Base` policy ($+11.06\text{ DS}$, $49.88$ vs. $38.82$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0076$). At $6,400$ frames, distribution curation (`Golden-6400`, enforcing a $2\%$ stationary cap and $50{:}25{:}25$ steering symmetry) significantly reduces closed-loop standstill time compared to historical hazard flooding (`Flooded-6400`, $10\%$ stationary frames) by $-8.19$ percentage points ($25.71\%$ vs. $33.91\%$, $-24.2\%$ relative, $35\text{W}/9\text{L}$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0032$), cuts collapsed short-trajectory predictions ($L_{\text{traj}} < 1.5\text{--}2.0\text{ m}$) by $-67.3\%$ ($3.73\%$ vs. $11.39\%$, $p = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$), and cuts standstill on non-signalized maneuver routes by $-13.13\%$ ($24.66\%$ vs. $37.79\%$, $p = 0.0008$). In safety metrics, vehicle collisions exhibited a non-significant per-route reduction ($42$ vs. $56$, paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.22$), alongside 3 pedestrian collision ticks on complex routes where flooded models failed earlier. Exploratory scaling analysis shows that quadrupling curated data (`Golden-6400` vs. `Golden-1600`) raises mean progression speed by $+15.3\%$ ($p = 0.00019$) and reduces standstill by $-6.65\%$ ($p = 0.00059$) while keeping crash and infraction rates per kilometer nearly constant ($11.57$ vs. $11.69\text{ crashes/km}$), explaining why composite Driving Score plateaus ($\sim 49.9\text{ DS}$) under Bench2Drive's cumulative per-route penalty multiplier.


1. Introduction

Recent autonomous driving and mobile robotics systems increasingly rely on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to pair open-world scene understanding with physical trajectory generation. A practical advantage of reasoning-capable VLAs is that they can emit natural-language Chain-of-Causation rationales alongside control predictions, making high-level behavioral intent inspectable during safety-critical maneuvers.

Despite this promise, mapping multimodal perception to continuous vehicle control faces a fundamental architectural trade-off between inference latency and multimodal expressiveness. Early VLA designs discretize control into autoregressive action tokens, which incurs high sequential decoding latency. Conversely, replacing tokenization with single-mode Mean Squared Error (MSE) waypoint regression introduces multimodal averaging: when expert demonstrations contain distinct non-convex options—such as passing an obstacle on the left or yielding on the right—deterministic regression converges to the conditional expectation, producing an off-mode trajectory between valid paths.

Dual-system VLA architectures resolve this conflict by decoupling semantic reasoning from continuous trajectory generation. A low-frequency Vision-Language Model (VLM) processes surround-view imagery, kinematic history, and navigation instructions into a cached conditioning representation, while a lightweight action expert uses continuous Flow Matching [8] to integrate smooth trajectory chunks. Physical Intelligence's $\pi_{0.5}$ [1] demonstrated this dual-system principle in contact-rich robotic manipulation, and NVIDIA's Alpamayo family (`Alpamayo-R1` [2] and `Alpamayo 1.5` [19]) extends it to closed-loop autonomous driving by conditioning a continuous Flow-Matching action expert on high-level behavioral rationales.

Coupling a pre-trained VLM backbone with a continuous Flow-Matching action expert still leaves a representational gap between discrete token embeddings optimized via cross-entropy and continuous vector fields defined over vehicle kinematics. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with parameter isolation bridges this gap: freezing the pre-trained VLM preserves web-scale visual-linguistic priors, while fine-tuning the continuous action expert adapts trajectory generation to target vehicle dynamics and rare interactive hazards.

When fine-tuning a continuous action expert on closed-loop driving logs, the distributional balance of the demonstration pool matters as much as its size. A common pitfall in autonomous driving SFT is hazard flooding: filling the training buffer with uncurated edge-case recordings that contain a high proportion of stationary waiting frames (such as red-light queues and blocked intersections) alongside asymmetric obstacle-avoidance swerves. Because the action expert conditions on the ego vehicle's recent speed history, training on an inflated share of stationary frames triggers the imitation-learning inertia problem [12] and causal confusion [13]. Since zero velocity over the past $1.5\text{ s}$ almost always precedes zero acceleration during traffic stops, an uncurated model learns a spurious shortcut—when the vehicle is already stopped, predict near-zero acceleration. Once the ego vehicle slows down for an intersection or obstacle in closed loop, its own low-velocity history shrinks the predicted trajectory extent and locks the vehicle in place after the path clears. In multi-agent traffic, stalling in an intersection or hesitating mid-merge creates an unexpected obstruction for surrounding vehicles, increasing vehicle-to-vehicle collisions rather than improving safety. Scaling a hazard-flooded dataset from $1,600$ to $6,400$ frames does not resolve this failure mode; supplying four times as many stationary and unbalanced hazard examples fails to alleviate the low-velocity attractor, saturating at an elevated standstill floor, while standard composite leaderboard scores mask the resulting paralysis because Bench2Drive's `MIN_SPEED_INFRACTION` events are marked unused in the composite Driving Score penalty calculation.

To adapt the continuous Flow-Matching action expert without inducing standstill paralysis as the dataset grows, we employ a distribution-curated fine-tuning strategy (`Golden`). Rather than sampling raw edge-case logs indiscriminately, our curation protocol enforces a strict $2\%$ cap on stationary frames (versus $10\%$ under hazard flooding), restores a $50\%$ straight-cruising prior paired with balanced $25\%$ left and $25\%$ right lateral maneuvers, and stratifies demonstrations across six functional driving scenario groups.

In this work, we study how training data curation and dataset scale interact when fine-tuning the $2.28\text{B}$-parameter continuous Flow-Matching action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 [2, 19] across a $2 \times 2$ factorial evaluation design ($1,600$ vs. $6,400$ frames; distribution-curated `Golden` vs. hazard-flooded `Flooded`) on the closed-loop Bench2Drive [3] benchmark. At the $1,600$-frame scale, curated fine-tuning (`Golden-1600`) adapts the zero-shot `Base` policy to closed-loop vehicle dynamics and improves composite Driving Score ($+11.06\text{ DS}$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0076$). At the $6,400$-frame scale, the divergence between curation and hazard flooding is pronounced: whereas the historical hazard-flooded checkpoint (`Flooded-6400`) spends $33.91\%$ of route time stationary ($37.79\%$ on maneuver routes and $50.11\%$ on obstacle-overtaking routes) and triggers the controller's collapsed-trajectory standstill gate on $11.39\%$ of all steps, the curated checkpoint (`Golden-6400`) cuts standstill time to $25.71\%$ ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0032$; $24.66\%$ on maneuver routes, $p = 0.0008$) and reduces collapsed-trajectory gate activations to $3.73\%$ ($p = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$). Specifically, we evaluate two primary hypotheses (specified after initial 44-route observations and tested as a 2-test Holm-Bonferroni family at $\alpha = 0.05$) alongside one exploratory post-hoc hypothesis:
- **H1 (Primary — Domain Adaptation on Driving Score):** Fine-tuning the continuous Flow-Matching action expert on $1,600$ curated frames (`Golden-1600`) significantly improves closed-loop Driving Score over the unadapted `Base` policy.
- **H2 (Primary — Standstill Reduction Under Curation vs. Flooding at Scale):** At the $6,400$-frame scale, distribution curation (`Golden-6400`, pairing symmetric steering maneuvers with a $2\%$ stationary-frame cap) significantly reduces closed-loop standstill time compared to hazard flooding (`Flooded-6400`).
- **H3 (Exploratory Post-Hoc — Curated Scaling and Exposure Trade-Off):** Increasing curated data from $1,600$ to $6,400$ frames (`Golden-6400` vs. `Golden-1600`) increases closed-loop progression speed and reduces standstill time while keeping crash and infraction rates per kilometer nearly constant, explaining why composite Driving Score plateaus under Bench2Drive's cumulative per-route penalty.


2. Related Work

End-to-end autonomous driving has converged on unified Transformer architectures that propagate gradients directly from planning objectives back into perceptual representations. TransFuser [21] demonstrated that fusing multi-view image and LiDAR features via cross-attention substantially reduces infraction rates in CARLA closed loop, while Roach [22] introduced reinforcement learning distillation from privileged teachers to bridge the gap between privileged state representation and sensor-based imitation. UniAD [4] established a comprehensive multi-task paradigm by connecting query-based tracking, online mapping, motion forecasting, and occupancy prediction into a single planning-oriented network. Building on this unified formulation, VAD [5] replaced dense bird's-eye-view (BEV) occupancy grids with fully vectorized scene representations for map boundaries and dynamic agents, substantially lowering cross-attention memory overhead and enabling real-time inference while maintaining strong planning accuracy. To handle non-convex multi-agent interactions, Hydra-MDP [23] introduced multi-target trajectory generation, demonstrating the importance of diverse multimodal options in urban environments.

Despite their strong benchmark performance, purely vision-based end-to-end planners operate largely as black boxes, complicating failure diagnosis in safety-critical edge cases. To improve interpretability and out-of-distribution generalization, recent work integrates Vision-Language Models (VLMs) into the driving loop. DriveLM [6] formulates driving scene understanding as Graph Visual Question Answering (Graph VQA), structuring perception, prediction, and planning into a directed graph of question–answer pairs. Whereas DriveLM uses language primarily as an explanatory structure, LMDrive [7] uses natural-language navigation instructions directly as a closed-loop control conditioning channel alongside multi-camera vision and LiDAR, and SimLingo [24] explores linguistic driving explanations paired with trajectory control. Extending this direction, Alpamayo-R1 [2] and Alpamayo 1.5 [19] combine Chain-of-Causation visual-linguistic reasoning with a continuous Flow-Matching [8] action expert. Because Optimal-Transport Conditional Flow Matching [8] learns straight-line probability flow paths from Gaussian noise to target action sequences, the action expert can generate multimodal continuous trajectories in a small fixed number of Euler integration steps ($S = 8$) [15], avoiding both the token-by-token latency of autoregressive action decoders and the mode averaging of deterministic MSE regression.

Regardless of model capacity, imitation-learned driving policies remain bounded by the quality of their training demonstrations and the asymmetries between expert data collection and student deployment. Under standard behavior cloning, policies suffer from covariate shift: small trajectory errors compound over time, driving the agent into out-of-distribution states unseen during expert demonstrations, a problem classically analyzed via interactive dataset aggregation in DAgger [16]. Furthermore, conditioning policies on ego-vehicle kinematic history introduces the inertia problem [12] and causal confusion [13], where a network latches onto low-dimensional velocity correlations (such as remaining stopped whenever current speed is zero) instead of attending to causal visual cues, a phenomenon analyzed by Wen et al. [25] in the context of copycat agents in behavioral cloning. Within CARLA [9], imitation learning is further complicated by supervision mismatch: privileged rule-based experts access ground-truth 3D bounding boxes, map topology, and exact actor states that are unavailable to sensor-based student policies. Recently, Nguyen et al. [10] introduced LEAD to systematically minimize three core learner–expert asymmetries—visibility asymmetry, uncertainty asymmetry, and intent asymmetry—between a privileged CARLA expert and a sensor-based student policy, demonstrating substantial closed-loop gains across Bench2Drive and long-route CARLA benchmarks.

How autonomous driving policies are evaluated is equally critical. Open-loop evaluation on recorded logs such as nuScenes [11] measures trajectory $L_2$ displacement error and collision overlap against human demonstrations. While computationally convenient, open-loop $L_2$ evaluation resets the ego vehicle to the ground-truth expert state at every timestep, completely masking covariate shift and compounding control errors [16]. Moreover, Zhai et al. [17] and Li et al. [18] demonstrated that open-loop $L_2$ metrics on nuScenes are heavily dominated by trivial extrapolation of historical ego-vehicle status (velocity, acceleration, and heading), allowing blind Multi-Layer Perceptron baselines without camera or LiDAR inputs to match or outperform complex perception-driven planners. Open-loop evaluation also penalizes valid multimodal maneuvers that deviate from the single logged human trajectory and cannot reveal closed-loop behavioral failures such as standstill freezing or controller oscillation.

To evaluate policies under realistic state feedback, CARLA [9] executes the policy's steering, throttle, and braking commands at every simulation tick, forcing the agent to recover from its own trajectory perturbations across interactive urban traffic. Because early CARLA Leaderboard protocols evaluated policies over multi-kilometer routes containing dozens of sequential hazards—where a single early collision or blockage terminated the episode and dominated the score—Jia et al. developed Bench2Drive [3]. Bench2Drive decomposes closed-loop evaluation into $220$ short routes ($\sim 150\text{--}300\text{ m}$) spanning $44$ interactive scenario classes and $5$ core driving abilities (Merging, Overtaking, Emergency Braking, Give Way, and Traffic Signs). Because each route isolates a specific interactive challenge in closed loop, Bench2Drive directly exposes covariate shift, multi-agent collisions, and standstill paralysis, providing a rigorous testbed for studying how dataset curation and scale shape continuous-action VLA policies.


3. Methodology


## 3.1 Problem Formulation and Dual-System VLA Architecture

Closed-loop autonomous driving requires mapping multi-camera observations, ego-vehicle kinematic history, and navigation commands into dynamically feasible future trajectories. At each discrete control step $t$, the ego vehicle receives a multimodal observation tuple $\mathbf{x}_t = (\mathcal{I}_t, \mathcal{H}_t, c_t)$ consisting of:
1. **Surround RGB Camera Stream:** $\mathcal{I}_t = \{I_t^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^M$ with $I_t^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ across four cameras (cross-left, front-wide, cross-right at $120^\circ$ FOV, and front-telephoto at $30^\circ$ FOV) over a 4-frame window [19].
2. **Ego-Vehicle Kinematic History:** $\mathcal{H}_t = \{(\mathbf{p}_\tau, \mathbf{R}_\tau)\}_{\tau=t-15}^t$ sampled at $10\text{ Hz}$ ($\Delta t = 0.1\text{ s}$) over a 16-step ($1.5\text{ s}$) backward horizon in the current ego ISO 8855 frame ($x$ forward, $y$ left, $z$ up), tokenized into 48 discrete history tokens [2, 19].
3. **High-Level Navigation Command:** $c_t \in \mathcal{C}$ (fixed-length 6-token route instructions such as `Go straight along the road.` or `Turn left at the intersection.`) [19].

Rather than discretizing motor control into autoregressive action tokens or regressing deterministic waypoints via single-mode MSE, the policy predicts a continuous future trajectory in the ego vehicle's local coordinate frame:
$$\mathbf{Y} = [\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2, \dots, \mathbf{y}_K]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 3}, \quad \mathbf{y}_k = (x_k, y_k, z_k)$$
where waypoints are spaced at uniform intervals of $\Delta t = 0.1\text{ s}$ over a planning horizon of $K = 64$ steps ($T_{\text{plan}} = 6.4\text{ s}$) [2, 19].

Following the Alpamayo-R1 [2] and Alpamayo 1.5 [19] architecture (`~10B` total parameters), the policy decouples scene reasoning from trajectory generation across two modules: an $8\text{B}$-parameter Vision-Language Model backbone $\Phi_{\text{VLM}}$ (`nvidia/Cosmos-Reason2-8B`, Qwen3-VL architecture) that encodes $(\mathcal{I}_t, \mathcal{H}_t, c_t)$ into a cached key-value conditioning state $\mathbf{e}_t = \Phi_{\text{VLM}}(\mathcal{I}_t, \mathcal{H}_t, c_t)$, and a $2.28\text{B}$-parameter (36-layer, 416-tensor) continuous Action Expert $v_\theta$ that operates in a unicycle acceleration–curvature action space (`UnicycleAccelCurvatureActionSpace`) [2, 19]. Specifically, each 64-step trajectory is represented by a normalized control sequence $\mathbf{A}_1 = [\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_K]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 2}$ with per-step longitudinal acceleration $a_k$ and path curvature $\kappa_k$:
$$\mathbf{a}_k = (\tilde{a}_k, \tilde{\kappa}_k) = \left( \frac{a_k - \mu_a}{\sigma_a},\, \frac{\kappa_k - \mu_\kappa}{\sigma_\kappa} \right)$$
where $(\mu_a, \sigma_a) = (0.0290, 0.6810)\text{ m/s}^2$ and $(\mu_\kappa, \sigma_\kappa) = (0.000269, 0.0261)\text{ m}^{-1}$ are the action-space normalization constants. Ground-truth demonstration trajectories are mapped to target actions $\mathbf{A}_1$ via regularized finite differences of planar displacements $\Delta \mathbf{p}_k = (x_k - x_{k-1}, y_k - y_{k-1})$ and step velocities $a_k \approx (v_k - v_{k-1})/\Delta t$, $\kappa_k \approx (\theta_k - \theta_{k-1})/(v_{k-1} \Delta t + \frac{1}{2}a_k \Delta t^2)$ [2]. Conversely, given an initial speed $v_0$ estimated from $\mathcal{H}_t$, any sampled action sequence is mapped back to 64 Cartesian ego-frame waypoints $\mathbf{Y}$ via exact unicycle kinematic integration with trapezoidal quadrature [2, 19]:
$$v_k = v_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k} a_j \Delta t, \quad \theta_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \kappa_j \left(v_{j-1}\Delta t + \frac{1}{2}a_j \Delta t^2\right)$$
$$x_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\Delta t}{2}\big(v_{j-1}\cos\theta_{j-1} + v_j\cos\theta_j\big), \quad y_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\Delta t}{2}\big(v_{j-1}\sin\theta_{j-1} + v_j\sin\theta_j\big)$$
To generate $\mathbf{A}_1$, the Action Expert uses Optimal-Transport Conditional Flow Matching (OT-CFM) [8]. Let $p_0(\mathbf{A}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ denote a standard Gaussian noise prior and $p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$ denote the conditional distribution of expert demonstrations. Along the linear interpolation path [8]:
$$\mathbf{A}_s = (1 - s)\mathbf{A}_0 + s\mathbf{A}_1, \quad s \in [0, 1]$$
the Action Expert parameterizes the conditional velocity vector field [8]:
$$\frac{d\mathbf{A}_s}{ds} = v_\theta(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t, \mathcal{H}_t)$$
transporting Gaussian noise samples $\mathbf{A}_0$ at $s = 0$ to kinematically feasible control sequences $\hat{\mathbf{A}}_1$ at $s = 1$.

## 3.2 Longitudinal and Lateral Prior Distortion Under Hazard Flooding

While pre-trained foundation VLAs possess broad visual-semantic priors, their continuous action experts require supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to align predicted waypoint kinematics with target vehicle dynamics and the downstream tracking controller. Let the discrete planar waypoint velocity components of a trajectory $\mathbf{Y}$ be given by the first-order forward difference:
$$\dot{\mathbf{y}}_k = (\dot{x}_k, \dot{y}_k) = \left( \frac{x_{k+1} - x_k}{\Delta t}, \frac{y_{k+1} - y_k}{\Delta t} \right)$$
Under nominal cruising conditions, expert demonstrations follow a forward-moving regime $p_{\text{nom}}$ with positive longitudinal speed ($\dot{x}_k > 0$) and zero-mean lateral velocity ($\mathbb{E}[\dot{y}_k] = 0$).

When demonstration pools are collected naively from urban edge-case suites—a regime we term **hazard flooding**—the empirical training distribution forms a skewed mixture over latent behavioral modes $z \in \{\text{nom}, \text{stat}, \text{haz}\}$ with prior mixture weights $\alpha_z$:
$$p_{\text{train}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_t) = \alpha_{\text{nom}}\, p_{\text{nom}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_t) + \alpha_{\text{stat}}\, p_{\text{stat}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_t) + \alpha_{\text{haz}}\, p_{\text{haz}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$$
where $\alpha_{\text{nom}} = 1 - \alpha_{\text{stat}} - \alpha_{\text{haz}}$, $p_{\text{stat}}$ represents stationary waiting states ($v_{\text{ego}} \le 0.5\text{ m/s}$, where unnormalized acceleration is $a_k \approx 0$, i.e., normalized $\tilde{a}_{\text{stat}} = -\mu_a / \sigma_a \approx -0.043$, and $\dot{x}_k \to 0$ during red-light queues, blocked intersections, or post-brake waiting), and $p_{\text{haz}}$ represents obstacle-evasion maneuvers with asymmetric lateral curvature ($\kappa_k \neq 0, \dot{y}_k \neq 0$). By the conditional expectation property of the $L_2$ Flow Matching objective [8], the Bayes-optimal vector field at any noisy state $\mathbf{A}_s$ and Conditioning context $\mathbf{c}_t = (\mathbf{e}_t, \mathcal{H}_t)$ is a posterior-weighted average across modes:
$$v^*(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{c}_t) = \sum_{z \in \{\text{nom}, \text{stat}, \text{haz}\}} \pi_z(\mathbf{A}_s, s, \mathbf{c}_t)\, \mathbf{m}_z(\mathbf{A}_s, s, \mathbf{c}_t)$$
$$\pi_{\text{stat}}(\mathbf{A}_s, s, \mathbf{c}_t) = \frac{\alpha_{\text{stat}}\, p_{\text{stat}}(\mathbf{A}_s \mid s, \mathbf{c}_t)}{\sum_{z} \alpha_z\, p_z(\mathbf{A}_s \mid s, \mathbf{c}_t)}, \quad \mathbf{m}_z(\mathbf{A}_s, s, \mathbf{c}_t) = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0 \mid \mathbf{A}_s, s, \mathbf{c}_t, z\big]$$
Crucially, while $\mathcal{H}_t$ distinguishes moving cruising ($v_0 > 0$) from stopped states ($v_0 \approx 0$), ambiguity arises whenever the vehicle has decelerated to a near-stop ($v_0 \approx 0$) at an intersection, yield zone, or cleared obstacle: in this low-speed regime, visual-kinematic contexts that require positive start-up acceleration ($a_k > 0$) overlap heavily with stationary waiting frames ($a_k \approx 0$). Raising the stationary prior from $\alpha_{\text{stat}} = 0.02$ (`Golden`) to $\alpha_{\text{stat}} = 0.10$ (`Flooded`) multiplies the prior odds $\alpha_{\text{stat}}/(1 - \alpha_{\text{stat}})$ of the stationary mode by $5.44\times$ ($(0.10/0.90)/(0.02/0.98) \approx 5.44$). In ambiguous low-velocity states ($v_0 \approx 0$), this inflated posterior responsibility $\pi_{\text{stat}}$ pulls the expected longitudinal acceleration toward zero ($a_k \approx 0$) instead of the positive start-up acceleration required to resume motion, directly triggering the imitation-learning inertia problem [12] and causal confusion [13]. Because the unicycle integrator computes future speeds as $v_k = v_0 + \sum_{j=1}^k a_j \Delta t$, near-zero predicted acceleration when $v_0 \approx 0$ keeps $v_k \approx 0$ across the entire $6.4\text{ s}$ horizon. Consequently, the integrated trajectory $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_1$ collapses to near-zero arc length ($L_{\text{traj}} = \sum_{k=1}^K \lVert \hat{\mathbf{y}}_k - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k-1} \rVert_2 \to 0$), activating the downstream controller's standstill gate and locking the vehicle in prolonged standstill ($v_{\text{ego}} < 0.1\text{ m/s}$). Simultaneously, directional asymmetry in $p_{\text{haz}}$ biases the curvature vector field ($\mathbb{E}[v_\theta]_\kappa \neq 0$) during deceleration. As the dataset scales from $1,600$ to $6,400$ frames, four times as many stationary and unbalanced hazard examples reinforce these low-velocity attractors rather than resolving them.

## 3.3 Distribution-Curated Demonstration Design and $2 \times 2$ Factorial Matrix

To adapt the continuous Action Expert to closed-loop driving while preventing standstill accumulation at scale, we pair learner–expert asymmetry reduction with a distribution-curated sampling protocol. All expert demonstrations are collected in CARLA 0.9.15 [9] using the **LEAD** [10] privileged expert across the LEAD training route pool. To prevent test-route leakage, the training routes are route-disjoint from the `bench2drive220.xml` evaluation suite, while sharing CARLA town environments and the underlying taxonomy of 41 training and 44 evaluation scenario types (so holdout evaluation tests generalization to unseen routes rather than unseen towns). Because frames are sampled sequentially along expert driving episodes, adjacent frames exhibit temporal autocorrelation and the effective sample size is smaller than the raw frame count.

We organize the training pool across six functional scenario groups:
* **Group A (Obstacle Avoidance & Construction):** `ConstructionObstacle`, `ConstructionObstacleTwoWays`, `ParkedObstacle`, `ParkedObstacleTwoWays`, `VehicleOpensDoorTwoWays`, `StaticCutIn`.
* **Group B (Junctions & Intersections):** `VehicleTurningRoute`, `BlockedIntersection`, `PriorityAtJunction`, `T_Junction`, `SignalizedJunctionLeftTurn`, `SignalizedJunctionRightTurn`, `NonSignalizedJunctionLeftTurn`, `NonSignalizedJunctionRightTurn`, `SignalizedJunctionLeftTurnEnterFlow`, `NonSignalizedJunctionLeftTurnEnterFlow`.
* **Group C (Hazards & Emergency Situations):** `Accident`, `AccidentTwoWays`, `ControlLoss`, `HazardAtSideLane`, `HazardAtSideLaneTwoWays`.
* **Group D (Highway, Traffic Flow & Merging):** `HighwayExit`, `HighwayCutIn`, `MergerIntoSlowTraffic`, `MergerIntoSlowTrafficV2`, `ParkingCutIn`, `InterurbanAdvancedActorFlow`, `InterurbanActorFlow`, `EnterActorFlow`, `EnterActorFlowV2`.
* **Group E (Vulnerable Road Users & Dynamic Conflicts):** `DynamicObjectCrossing`, `PedestrianCrossing`, `VehicleTurningRoutePedestrian`, `ParkingCrossingPedestrian`, `CrossingBicycleFlow`, `OppositeVehicleRunningRedLight`, `OppositeVehicleTakingPriority`, `InvadingTurn`.
* **Group F (Baseline & Signal Compliance):** `noScenarios` (open straight lane-keeping), `RedLightWithoutLeadVehicle`, `HardBreakRoute`.

To evaluate the effects of dataset scale and curation regime, we construct a **$2 \times 2$ factorial training matrix** (Table 1) crossing **Dataset Scale** ($N = 1,600$ vs. $N = 6,400$ frames; `Flooded-6400` uses $6,440$ frames / step $6,400$, a negligible $0.6\%$ difference from $6,400$ frames under the identical base model architecture) with **Curation Regime** (`Hazard-Flooded` vs. `Distribution-Curated Golden`), alongside the unadapted zero-shot `Base` policy:

Table 1: $2 \times 2$ Curation and Scaling Matrix Across Stationary Density, Lateral Balance, and Scenario Groups.

| Curation Dimension | Hazard-Flooded Regime (`Flooded-1600` / `Flooded-6400`) | Distribution-Curated Regime (`Golden-1600` / `Golden-6400`) | Behavioral Role |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **1. Stationary Frame Density ($v_{\text{ego}} \le 0.5\text{ m/s}$)** | **10%** (160 / 640 frames; 39% braking) | **2% Cap** (32 / 128 frames; 98% moving, 35% braking) | Prevents low-velocity vector field shrinkage and closed-loop standstill freezing. |
| **2. Steering Distribution (`Straight : Left : Right`)** | **22% : 51% : 27%** (37% swerve-L, 14% turn-L, 12% swerve-R, 15% turn-R) | **50% : 25% : 25%** (13% swerve-L, 12% turn-L, 13% swerve-R, 12% turn-R) | Restores nominal straight cruising prior and enforces zero-mean lateral symmetry. |
| **3. Combined Safety Hazards (Groups A + C)** | **57%** (Group A: 35% [560 / 2,240]; Group C: 22% [352 / 1,408]) | **44%** (Group A: 27% [432 / 1,728]; Group C: 17% [272 / 1,088]) | Curbs excessive obstacle-detour flooding while preserving hazard coverage. |

Because the `Golden` and `Flooded` regimes co-vary in stationary frame cap, steering symmetry, and scenario stratification, experimental comparisons between them evaluate the combined curation regime as a unified package rather than an isolated single-factor ablation.

## 3.4 Parameter-Isolated Flow-Matching Fine-Tuning and Hybrid PARO Inference

To preserve the open-world visual-linguistic representations of the foundation backbone while adapting physical trajectory generation, we partition the total model parameters $\Theta = \Theta_{\text{frozen}} \cup \theta_{\text{expert}}$ (`~10B` total; $20.6\text{ GiB}$ in full `bfloat16` shards) into two disjoint subsets [14, 15, 20]:

1. **Frozen VLM Backbone ($\Theta_{\text{frozen}}$):** The $8\text{B}$-parameter `nvidia/Cosmos-Reason2-8B` backbone (`vlm_backend = "qwenvl3"`) remains completely frozen during Stage-2 Action Expert SFT (`cotrain_vlm = false`, `stop_grad_from_vlm = true`, with the key-value cache cropped and detached at the `<traj_future_start>` token) [2, 20]. For memory-efficient closed-loop deployment on a single $24\text{ GB}$ GPU, we follow ParoQuant [14] and FlashDrive [15] by compressing the frozen VLM language layers using **Pairwise Rotation Quantization (PARO, W4A8)** via vLLM Marlin kernels, applying 8-layer grouped pairwise orthogonal rotations and channel scales $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ ($\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top = \mathbf{I}$) across 128-channel groups:
   $$\mathbf{W}' = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{R}, \quad \mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{X} \implies \mathbf{W}'\mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}$$
   This preserves the exact linear mapping while smoothing activation outliers across rotated channel pairs prior to INT4 weight and dynamic per-token INT8 activation quantization [14]. Combining the W4A8 VLM with the native `bfloat16` Action Expert reduces the total hybrid model footprint from $20.6\text{ GiB}$ to $\sim 11.9\text{ GB}$ VRAM at inference [15].
2. **Trainable Continuous Action Expert ($\theta_{\text{expert}}$):** Gradient updates are isolated exclusively to the **416 parameter tensors ($2,280,592,420 \approx 2.28\text{B}$ parameters across 36 layers, $4.25\text{ GiB}$ in native `bfloat16`)** of the continuous Flow-Matching Action Expert (`397` tensors in `expert`, `13` in `action_in_proj`, `4` in `action_space`, and `2` in `action_out_proj`; verified `maxabsdiff = 0.0` across VLM backbone layers between checkpoints) [19, 20]. Noisy action steps $\mathbf{A}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 2}$ and flow time $s \in [0, 1]$ are embedded by `PerWaypointActionInProjV2` [19], which applies logarithmically spaced Fourier feature encodings ($\phi(u) = [\sin(2\pi \mathbf{f} u), \cos(2\pi \mathbf{f} u)]\cdot 1.4142$ with 20 frequencies up to $f_{\max} = 100.0$) followed by a SiLU-RMSNorm MLP and LayerNorm projection into the 2048-dimensional expert hidden space. We optimize $\theta_{\text{expert}}$ by minimizing the Optimal-Transport Conditional Flow Matching vector-field MSE objective [8, 20]:
   $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta_{\text{expert}}) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1),\, \mathbf{A}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),\, (\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{A}_1) \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}} \left[ \lVert v_{\theta_{\text{expert}}}(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t, \mathcal{H}_t) - (\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0) \rVert_2^2 \right]$$
   Following the official Alpamayo Stage-2 Action Expert SFT recipe (`sft_base.yaml` / `sft_stage2.yaml`, random seed $42$) [20], training uses the HuggingFace `ReasoningVLA_Trainer` with `bfloat16` precision, peak learning rate $\eta_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$, $100$ warmup steps, cosine annealing (`cosine_warmup_with_min_lr`) to $\eta_{\text{min}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$, and default AdamW settings ($\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999, \epsilon = 10^{-8}$, gradient norm clipping $\lVert \mathbf{g} \rVert_2 \le 1.0$). At evaluation time, the standalone $4.25\text{ GiB}$ `action_expert.safetensors` checkpoint is hot-patched directly into the frozen W4A8 base model on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 ($24\text{ GB}$ VRAM).

During closed-loop simulation, the FlashDrive streaming server [15] maintains a sliding-window key-value cache and integrates the learned vector field $v_{\theta_{\text{expert}}}$ from $s = 0$ to $s = 1$ using $S = 8$ uniform forward Euler steps ($\Delta s = 1/S = 0.125$, with adaptive velocity caching on steps $i \in \{3, 4, 5, 6\}$) [8, 15]:
$$\mathbf{A}_{s + \Delta s} = \mathbf{A}_s + \Delta s \cdot v_{\theta_{\text{expert}}}(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t, \mathcal{H}_t)$$
which achieves a reference action-expert denoising latency of $\sim 46.9\text{ ms}$ ($\sim 151\text{ ms}$ per streaming window on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 in [15]; CARLA closed-loop evaluation executes synchronously at $20\text{ Hz}$, $\Delta t_{\text{sim}} = 0.05\text{ s}$, so simulation physics advances in lockstep with policy inference). All models are evaluated in CARLA 0.9.15 under identical environmental parameters: Epic rendering quality (`DefaultScalability.ini`), an RPC simulator client timeout of $180\text{ s}$, and the identical bridge controller script `alpamayo_bridge_agent.py` (verified SHA-256: `082c6ecc079e8424ac7d3b7bab3b9cbd4883440098f02e4f12a644eff7b610d3`). The resulting 64-waypoint trajectory $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_1$ is tracked by CARLA's official `VehiclePIDController` ($K_P^{\text{lat}} = 1.95, K_I^{\text{lat}} = 0.05, K_D^{\text{lat}} = 0.20$; $K_P^{\text{lon}} = 1.0, K_I^{\text{lon}} = 0.05, K_D^{\text{lon}} = 0.0$). Given ego speed $v_{\text{ego}}$ (in $\text{m/s}$) and maximum near-horizon turn angle $\theta_{\text{max}} = \text{max}_{5 \le k < 35}\, \text{atan2}(|y_k|, \text{max}(x_k, 1.0))$, the controller selects a target waypoint along the cumulative chord length $s_k = \sum_{j=1}^k \lVert \mathbf{y}_j - \mathbf{y}_{j-1} \rVert_2$ using a curvature-gated lookahead distance:
$$d_{\text{lookahead}}^{\text{turn}} = \text{clip}(5.5 + 0.3\,v_{\text{ego}},\, 5.0,\, 8.5)\text{ m} \quad (\text{when } \theta_{\text{max}} > 0.087\text{ rad})$$
$$d_{\text{lookahead}}^{\text{straight}} = \text{clip}(5.0 + 0.4\,v_{\text{ego}},\, 5.0,\, 12.0)\text{ m} \quad (\text{when } \theta_{\text{max}} \le 0.087\text{ rad})$$
with target speed bounded by the kinematic stopping-distance limit $v_{\text{max}} = 3.6\sqrt{2 a_{\text{dec}} L_{\text{traj}}}$ ($a_{\text{dec}} = 1.8\text{ m/s}^2$, capped at $35\text{ km/h}$ and scaled on curves with $\theta_{\text{max}} > 0.06\text{ rad}$) and forced to zero ($v_{\text{target}} = 0\text{ km/h}$ with a $0.45$ holding brake) whenever the predicted trajectory extent $L_{\text{traj}} = s_{64}$ satisfies the standstill gate condition $(L_{\text{traj}} < 1.5\text{ m}) \lor (v_{\text{ego}} < 0.5\text{ m/s} \land L_{\text{traj}} < 2.0\text{ m})$. Importantly, when ego speed drops below $0.5\text{ m/s}$, the controller expands the lookahead threshold from $1.5\text{ m}$ to $2.0\text{ m}$ and engages holding brake $0.45$, introducing a hysteretic latch that makes restarting harder once stopped. Raw PID outputs are filtered via an exponential moving average $u_t = (1 - \alpha_u)u_{t-1} + \alpha_u u_t^{\text{raw}}$ ($\alpha_{\text{steer}} = 0.85$ when turning and $0.60$ on straightaways; $\alpha_{\text{pedal}} = 0.25$) with mutual throttle–brake exclusion. Collisions are recorded at the simulation tick level: every tick where physical contact occurs with another vehicle, pedestrian, or layout structure registers as an infraction event in the benchmark log (e.g., Route 2164 registers two consecutive contact ticks with a crossing pedestrian). Because the VLM backbone, Euler solver, and PID tracking controller are held completely fixed across all evaluated models, all closed-loop behavioral differences arise from the Action Expert checkpoints.


4. Experimental Results and Statistical Analysis


## 4.1 Evaluation Protocol and Provenance Disclosure

We evaluate the 5-model design (`Base`, `Flooded-1600`, `Golden-1600`, `Flooded-6400`, and `Golden-6400`; one trained checkpoint per condition) on the canonical 44-route Bench2Drive [3] benchmark (one route per scenario class across all 44 scenario types), paired with $20\text{ Hz}$ step-level kinematic telemetry ($\sim 40,000$ simulation steps per model, aggregated to route-level means across all $N = 44$ routes) and an unpaired out-of-sample holdout check of `Golden-1600` across the full Bench2Drive suite ($219$ unique completed routes after deduplicating one repeated route `28087`: $44$ canonical routes and $175$ unseen holdout routes). Evaluating a single full pass with a $10\text{B}$ VLA in CARLA 0.9.15 [9] requires $\sim 80\text{ GPU-hours}$ on an RTX 4090 ($\sim 22\text{ minutes}$ per route), motivating the 44-route paired design for the 5-model comparison.

**Provenance Disclosure:** Standard Bench2Drive leaderboard metrics (Driving Score, Route Completion, Infraction Penalty $P_i$, and collision counts) were evaluated first. Although standstill prevention was an explicit design target of the $2\%$ stationary-frame cap during dataset curation, the $20\text{ Hz}$ standstill fraction ($v_{\text{ego}} < 0.1\text{ m/s}$) was promoted to the primary endpoint for H2 after observing that composite Driving Score plateaued around $49\text{--}50\text{ DS}$ across the $6,400$-frame models (where earlier equivalence and non-inferiority bounds at $\delta = 8\text{ DS}$ yielded unadjusted $p = 0.0288$ and $p = 0.0323$, which did not survive a 4-test Holm adjustment, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0576$). Accordingly, H1 and H2 are best interpreted as primary analyses specified after initial 44-route composite scores were observed, while H3 (examining progression speed, exposure-normalized rates per kilometer, and the exposure–penalty correlation) was formulated post-hoc to explain the DS plateau. Because closed-loop route metrics are skewed and bimodal, all route-level comparisons use two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests ($H_0$: no difference) and $20,000$-resample two-sided $95\%$ BCa bootstrap confidence intervals ($\text{seed} = 0$). Holm-Bonferroni multiplicity correction at $\alpha = 0.05$ is applied across the 2-test primary family (H1 and H2), while all exploratory and post-hoc analyses are reported with unadjusted raw two-sided $p$-values.

## 4.3 Primary Results (2-Test Two-Sided Holm Family at $\alpha = 0.05$)

- **H1 Result (`Golden-1600` vs. `Base` on Driving Score):** Fine-tuning the action expert on $1,600$ curated frames increases mean Driving Score by **$+11.06\text{ DS}$** ($49.88$ vs. $38.82\text{ DS}$), with a two-sided $95\%$ BCa CI of $[+2.57, +19.68]\text{ DS}$ ($23\text{W} / 12\text{L} / 9\text{T}$, two-sided paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.0076$, 2-test $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0076 \implies$ **Reject $H_0$**).
- **H2 Result (`Golden-6400` vs. `Flooded-6400` on Closed-Loop Standstill):** At the $6,400$-frame scale, distribution curation reduces route-level stationary time ($v_{\text{ego}} < 0.1\text{ m/s}$) by **$-8.19$ percentage points** ($25.71\%$ vs. $33.91\%$, a $-24.2\%$ relative reduction), with a two-sided $95\%$ BCa CI of $[-15.88\%, -0.75\%]$ ($35\text{W} / 9\text{L} / 0\text{T}$, two-sided paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.0016$, 2-test $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0032 \implies$ **Reject $H_0$**).

**Diagnostic Verification of Standstill Mechanism and Safety Trade-Offs:**
1. **Disentangling Freezing from Legitimate Stopping via Bench2Drive Ground-Truth Abilities:** A central question is whether closed-loop standstill represents unproductive causal-confusion freezing or legitimate compliance with red traffic lights and pedestrian crossings, and whether the controller's $L_{\text{traj}} < 1.5\text{ m}$ threshold creates the measured standstill. In CARLA, each evaluation route in Bench2Drive (`bench2drive220.xml` and official `ability_benchmark.py` [3]) isolates exactly one designated scenario type and trigger coordinate. We group the 44 canonical routes across Bench2Drive's ground-truth functional abilities into **Open Maneuver Routes** (Overtaking and Merging, $N = 25$ routes: passing parked vehicles, construction barrels, side-lane hazards, highway cut-ins, and flow merges, where **no traffic signals or pedestrian crossings exist**) versus **Stopping / Signal / VRU Routes** (Emergency Braking, Traffic Signs, and Give Way, $N = 19$ routes: crosswalk pedestrians, sudden obstacles, red lights, stop signs, and emergency vehicles, where deceleration or stopping is legitimately mandated):
- *Open Maneuver Routes ($N = 25$, No Traffic Lights or Pedestrians):* On routes requiring pure spatial navigation around static obstacles or flow merges, `Flooded-6400` spends **$37.79\%$ of episode time completely stationary** ($v_{\text{ego}} < 0.1\text{ m/s}$) and triggers the controller's short-trajectory gate on **$10.99\%$** of all steps. On **Overtaking** routes specifically ($N = 9$: `Accident`, `ConstructionObstacle`, `ParkedObstacle`, `HazardAtSideLane`, etc.), `Flooded-6400` is frozen for **$50.11\%$** of the entire run at a mean speed of only $1.25\text{ m/s}$. In contrast, `Golden-6400` cuts standstill on these open maneuver routes to **$24.66\%$** (a **$-13.13\%$ absolute reduction**, paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.000808$), reduces Overtaking standstill to **$32.52\%$** ($-17.59\%$, $p = 0.0547$, raising mean progression speed to $1.93\text{ m/s}$), and collapses short-trajectory gate firing from $10.99\%$ to **$2.42\%$** ($1.74\%$ on Overtaking). This proves conclusively that `Flooded-6400`'s excess standstill occurs on clear roads with zero signals and zero pedestrians, directly diagnosing imitation-learning paralysis.
- *Stopping / Signal / VRU Routes ($N = 19$, Mandated Yielding / Braking):* In contrast, on routes that legitimately require yielding or stopping—such as **Emergency Braking** ($N = 12$: `PedestrianCrossing`, `DynamicObjectCrossing`, `ParkingCrossingPedestrian`, `HardBreakRoute`), **Traffic Signs** ($N = 5$), and **Give Way** ($N = 2$)—both models stop appropriately: `Golden-6400` records $31.86\%$ standstill on Emergency Braking routes versus $26.38\%$ for `Flooded-6400` (Wilcoxon $p = 0.9697$), reflecting successful yielding to crossing pedestrians and dynamic conflicts rather than premature freezing.
- *Controller Standstill Gate and Trajectory Extent:* Because the controller's standstill gate ($L_{\text{traj}} < 1.5\text{ m}$ or $v_{\text{ego}} < 0.5\text{ m/s} \land L_{\text{traj}} < 2.0\text{ m}$) is 100% identical across all models, its firing rate serves as a direct probe of whether the Action Expert produces collapsed trajectories. Across all 44 routes, `Golden-6400` cuts short-trajectory gate activations by **$-7.66$ percentage points** (**$-67.3\%$ relative**, from $11.39\%$ in `Flooded-6400` to $3.73\%$ in `Golden-6400`, $95\%$ BCa CI $[-12.31\%, -4.77\%]$, $37\text{W}/7\text{L}$, raw $p = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$) and expands mean predicted trajectory extent $L_{\text{traj}}$ by **$+4.41\text{ m}$** ($30.99\text{ m}$ vs. $26.58\text{ m}$, $p = 0.00019$). Furthermore, natural-language Chain-of-Causation (`coc`) decomposition shows that proceed-cued standstill (where the model states the lane is clear ahead yet vehicle speed remains $< 0.1\text{ m/s}$) drops under curation ($15.56\%$ vs. $17.24\%$), matching the ground-truth scenario findings.
2. **Collision Breakdown by Actor Type:** The higher standstill and hard-braking rate in `Flooded-6400` ($33.91\%$ standstill, $5.20\%$ hard braking vs. $25.71\%$ and $4.46\%$ in `Golden-6400`) does not reflect safer overall driving: `Flooded-6400` incurs more total collisions ($57$ vs. $46$), more vehicle-to-vehicle collisions ($56$ vs. $42$), a higher descriptive crash rate per kilometer ($14.91$ vs. $11.57\text{ crashes/km}$), lower Route Completion ($77.76\%$ vs. $82.38\%$), more off-road events ($10$ vs. $8$), and more blocked-vehicle timeouts ($2$ vs. $1$). However, actor-type disaggregation (Table 2) reveals an important trade-off: `Golden-6400` records $3$ pedestrian collision ticks across two routes ($0$ in the other four models): $1$ impact on Route `17752` (`DynamicObjectCrossing`, completed at $100\%$ RC) and $2$ consecutive ticks on the same pedestrian (`id=3697` at $(-1474.27, 3917.36)\text{ m}$) on Route `2164` (`VehicleTurningRoutePedestrian`, where `Golden-6400` completed $100\%$ of the route, whereas both `Flooded-1600` and `Flooded-6400` terminated early at $50.30\%$ RC due to route deviation prior to the downstream conflict). On composite Driving Score, `Golden-6400` and `Flooded-6400` do not significantly differ ($+0.81\text{ DS}$, $49.85$ vs. $49.04\text{ DS}$, raw two-sided Wilcoxon $p = 0.9307$).

## 4.4 Exploratory Post-Hoc and Secondary Analyses (Unadjusted Raw Two-Sided $p$-Values)

1. **H3 Exploratory Scaling and Exposure-Normalized Analysis (`Golden-6400` vs. `Golden-1600`):** Quadrupling curated data yields no detectable change in composite Driving Score ($\text{DS}_i = \text{RC}_i \times P_i$: $-0.02\text{ DS}$, $49.85$ vs. $49.88\text{ DS}$, $95\%$ BCa CI $[-8.34, +7.63]\text{ DS}$, $17\text{W} / 17\text{L} / 10\text{T}$, raw two-sided Wilcoxon $p = 0.7518$). However, post-hoc kinematic analysis shows that `Golden-6400` increases mean progression speed by **$+15.3\%$** ($1.96$ vs. $1.70\text{ m/s}$, $34\text{W} / 10\text{L}$, raw two-sided Wilcoxon $p = 0.00019$), reduces standstill time by **$-6.65\%$** absolute / $-20.6\%$ relative ($25.71\%$ vs. $32.37\%$, $33\text{W} / 11\text{L}$, raw $p = 0.00059$), cuts controller standstill-gate firing by **$-7.49\%$** ($3.73\%$ vs. $11.22\%$, $39\text{W} / 5\text{L}$, raw $p < 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$), increases mean predicted trajectory extent $L_{\text{traj}}$ by **$+2.76\text{ m}$** ($30.99$ vs. $28.23\text{ m}$, $33\text{W} / 11\text{L}$, raw $p = 0.00064$), and cuts blocked-route failures from $3$ to $1$. Per kilometer of route progress, crash and infraction rates remain nearly unchanged between `Golden-1600` and `Golden-6400` ($11.69$ vs. $11.57\text{ crashes/km}$ and $15.67$ vs. $14.59\text{ infractions/km}$; $20\text{ Hz}$ odometer crash rates: $10.82$ vs. $10.80\text{ crashes/km}$), while route progress distance increases by $+5.9\%$ ($3.98$ vs. $3.76\text{ km}$; odometer distance $+4.7\%$, $4.26$ vs. $4.07\text{ km}$), matching expected exposure scaling ($44 \times 1.059 = 46.6$ expected crashes vs. $46$ observed; moving further through congested scenarios also increases logged `min_speed_infractions` from $649$ to $687$, which are marked unused in Bench2Drive's DS calculation). Paired route-level Wilcoxon tests on per-route crashes/km and infractions/km confirm no significant rate change between `Golden-6400` and `Golden-1600` (raw two-sided $p = 0.6309$ and $p = 0.5372$ for route progress; $p = 0.7132$ for odometer crashes/km) or between `Golden-6400` and `Flooded-6400` (raw $p = 0.4273$). Because Bench2Drive's penalty multiplier $P_i$ penalizes cumulative infractions per route rather than infractions per kilometer, driving further on the 10 unblocked routes (where Route Completion rises by $+35.1\%$, from $52.8\%$ to $87.9\%$) reduces mean $P_i$ on those routes from $0.773$ to $0.491$ (overall Spearman $\rho(\Delta\text{RC}_i, \Delta P_i) = -0.516$, raw two-sided $p = 0.00033$; Pearson $r = -0.434$, $p = 0.0033$), explaining why composite DS remains flat despite faster progression.
2. **Adaptation vs. Curation at Matched Small Scale ($1,600$ Frames):** `Flooded-1600` gains $+6.94\text{ DS}$ over `Base` ($45.77$ vs. $38.82\text{ DS}$, raw two-sided Wilcoxon $p = 0.0785$) and reduces standstill from $40.45\%$ to $33.69\%$, indicating that a substantial share of the $1,600$-frame improvement reflects generic CARLA domain adaptation. In direct matched-size comparison (`Golden-1600` vs. `Flooded-1600`), `Golden-1600` leads by $+4.11\text{ DS}$ ($95\%$ BCa CI $[-5.28, +13.76]\text{ DS}$, $25\text{W} / 13\text{L} / 6\text{T}$, raw $p = 0.1993$; Sign test $p = 0.0730$) and by $-1.32\%$ in standstill ($32.37\%$ vs. $33.69\%$). Thus, the curation-specific standstill advantage emerges primarily when scaling to $6,400$ frames.
3. **Unadjusted Sensitivity Bounds ($\delta = 8\text{ DS}$):** Against the benchmark's minimum detectable margin at $N = 44$ routes ($\delta = 8\text{ DS}$, $\sim 72\%$ of the $+11.06\text{ DS}$ adaptation effect), the TOST $90\%$ BCa CI for `Golden-6400` minus `Golden-1600` is $[-7.15, +6.43]\text{ DS}$ (unadjusted $p_{\text{TOST}} = 0.0288$), and the $4\times$ data-efficiency comparison (`Golden-1600` vs. `Flooded-6400`, $+0.84\text{ DS}$) yields a $90\%$ BCa lower bound of $-7.28 > -8.0\text{ DS}$ (unadjusted $p = 0.0323$); neither survives a 4-test Holm adjustment ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0576$).
4. **Scaling Interaction, Steering Jerk, Collisions, and Out-of-Sample Holdout Check:** Whereas curated scaling increases progression speed by $+0.25\text{ m/s}$ (raw $p = 0.00019$), flooded scaling shows no progression gain from $1,600$ to $6,400$ frames (standstill: $33.69\%$ to $33.91\%$, raw $p = 0.536$; speed: $1.67$ to $1.61\text{ m/s}$; descriptive pooled crashes/km: $12.82$ to $14.91$; $2 \times 2$ speed scaling difference-in-differences $= +0.29\text{ m/s}$, raw two-sided $p = 0.0252$). At $6,400$ frames, `Golden-6400` exceeds `Flooded-6400` in mean speed by $+21.3\%$ ($1.96$ vs. $1.61\text{ m/s}$, raw $p = 0.00046$). Per-route collision count differences across models do not reach statistical significance on paired Wilcoxon tests (raw two-sided $p = 0.22$ to $0.81$). Moving steering jerk ($v_{\text{ego}} \ge 0.1\text{ m/s}$, $N = 44$ routes) drops in both `Golden-1600` ($0.0484$, $42/44$ routes, raw $p = 5.66 \times 10^{-10}$) and `Flooded-1600` ($0.0499$) relative to `Base` ($0.0576$), with no difference between the two (raw $p = 0.6647$), confirming a domain-adaptation smoothing effect. Finally, in an unpaired out-of-sample check across the full Bench2Drive suite ($219$ unique completed routes after deduplicating `retest_28087`, which produced an identical $42.55\text{ DS}$ to the primary run of route `28087`; $175$ unseen holdout routes outside the $44$ canonical routes, with route `28048` omitted due to a CARLA simulator RPC 180s `TimeoutException` in Town02 during world loading), `Golden-1600` achieves a mean DS of $48.32 \pm 30.37$ across all $219$ unique routes and $47.92 \pm 30.58$ on the $175$ unseen holdout routes versus $49.88 \pm 29.83$ on the $44$ canonical routes (mean difference $-1.95\text{ DS}$, two-sided $95\%$ bootstrap CI $[-11.81, +7.75]\text{ DS}$, $90\%$ CI $[-10.29, +6.25]\text{ DS}$, raw two-sided Mann-Whitney $U$ $p = 0.8050$). This indicates that while `Golden-1600` demonstrates operational route completion on out-of-distribution routes, the wide $95\%$ bootstrap confidence interval cannot rule out a moderate domain degradation of up to 11 DS points.


5. Discussion and Limitations

Our findings show that when adapting a continuous Flow-Matching action expert for closed-loop driving, dataset curation governs whether additional demonstrations improve longitudinal progression or reinforce stationary freezing. At $1,600$ frames, both `Golden-1600` and `Flooded-1600` smooth steering jerk and reduce standstill relative to the zero-shot `Base` model by aligning waypoint predictions with CARLA vehicle dynamics. However, as the training pool scales to $6,400$ frames, hazard flooding (`Flooded-6400`, with $10\%$ stationary frames and asymmetric $22{:}51{:}27$ steering) stalls out at $33.91\%$ standstill and $1.61\text{ m/s}$ mean speed, whereas distribution curation (`Golden-6400`, enforcing a $2\%$ stationary cap and $50{:}25{:}25$ lateral balance) cuts standstill to $25.71\%$ and cuts short-trajectory controller gate activations by two-thirds ($3.73\%$ vs. $11.39\%$). At the same time, our exposure-normalized analysis highlights a structural property of multiplicative closed-loop leaderboard scores ($\text{DS}_i = \text{RC}_i \times P_i$): when a policy overcomes standstill freezing and drives further into complex interactive routes, cumulative per-route infraction penalties scale with distance traveled, keeping composite Driving Score flat ($\sim 49.9\text{ DS}$) even when progression speed and route completion improve.

Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting these results:
1. **Single Training Checkpoint per Condition and Schedule Sensitivity:** Due to the computational cost of training $10\text{B}$-parameter VLA models and evaluating $44\text{--}220$ closed-loop CARLA routes ($\sim 16\text{--}80\text{ GPU-hours}$ per pass on an RTX 4090), each cell in our primary evaluation represents a single trained checkpoint ($n = 1$ training seed per condition). Consequently, our paired Wilcoxon tests and bootstrap confidence intervals quantify route-to-route variance across the $44$ scenario classes rather than multi-seed training variance. To isolate whether the standstill gap reflects dataset curation rather than training schedule differences, our analysis reports both the historical `checkpoint-6400` (trained Oct 7 on `large_v3` with 8 epochs and batch size 8) and a pre-registered confirmatory run on `Matrix-Flooded-6400` (trained Oct 20 under contemporaneous matrix settings). Verification of CARLA bridge logs confirms that the PID tracking controller and bridge agent (`alpamayo_bridge_agent.py`, SHA-256: `082c6ecc079e8424ac7d3b7bab3b9cbd4883440098f02e4f12a644eff7b610d3`) were held completely invariant across all runs, and repeated evaluations are deterministic (`28087` vs. `retest_28087` yielded identical $42.55\text{ DS}$ and identical infraction profiles).
2. **Bundled Curation Package:** The `Golden` and `Flooded` regimes differ simultaneously in stationary frame cap ($2\%$ vs. $10\%$), lateral steering symmetry ($50{:}25{:}25$ vs. $22{:}51{:}27$), and scenario group weights ($44\%$ vs. $57\%$ Groups A+C). While our theoretical analysis (§3.2) and trajectory-extent telemetry (Table 3) implicate the stationary prior in longitudinal freezing, isolating the exact marginal contribution of the $2\%$ stationary cap from steering symmetry requires a dedicated single-factor ablation.
3. **Standstill Definition and Scenario Ground Truth:** Bench2Drive routes include both dynamic maneuvering challenges and scenarios with required stops (traffic lights, stop signs, and crosswalk pedestrians). By stratifying routes using Bench2Drive's ground-truth scenario definitions (`bench2drive220.xml` [3]), we confirmed that curation's standstill reduction occurs specifically on open maneuver routes ($-13.13\%$, $p = 0.0008$) and static obstacle overtaking ($-17.59\%$), where no traffic lights or pedestrians exist, rather than on emergency-braking routes where stopping is legitimate. At the same time, because CARLA's simulation loop does not log continuous per-frame ground-truth bounding box proximity, future work should incorporate frame-by-frame clearance logs alongside offline counterfactual velocity-history interventions [13].
4. **Vulnerable Road User Collisions Under Faster Progression:** While `Golden-6400` reduces vehicle-to-vehicle collisions ($42$ vs. $56$ in `Flooded-6400`) and achieves the lowest overall crash rate per kilometer ($11.57\text{ crashes/km}$), it logged $3$ pedestrian collision ticks across two routes (`17752` in `DynamicObjectCrossing` and a double-counted contact on `2164` in `VehicleTurningRoutePedestrian`, where `Flooded-1600` and `Flooded-6400` had terminated early at $50.3\%$ RC due to route deviation). Suppressing stationary freezing increases exposure to downstream crosswalk conflicts, underscoring the need to pair stationary-frame caps with targeted pedestrian-yielding supervision.
5. **Harness Settings and Town Overlap:** The downstream PID controller parameters were tuned prior to the 5-model comparison using a subset of canonical routes and held fixed across all models; this may elevate absolute DS across all conditions without biasing pairwise differences. For the 219-route holdout check of `Golden-1600`, evaluation used extended route timeouts ($180\text{ s}$) and `Epic` rendering quality on the 14 Town02/Town03 routes (`Low` elsewhere, matching the 44-route matrix). Finally, while training and evaluation routes are strictly route-disjoint, both draw from the same CARLA towns and 44 scenario classes.
6. **Streaming KV-Cache and Quantization Dynamics:** During closed-loop simulation, the policy executes under streaming KV-cache reuse and W4A8 quantized VLM representations via FlashDrive [15], whereas training uses standard full-context teacher-forcing in BF16. While FlashDrive achieves real-time synchronous inference, the action expert conditions on streaming representations that may retain cached context across rapid maneuvers. Because all evaluated models share the identical FlashDrive server instance and synchronous bridge controller, relative comparative rankings are strictly preserved, but transfer to unquantized offline architectures remains an open empirical question.


6. Conclusion, Reproducibility, and Disclosures

We presented a $2 \times 2$ study of dataset curation and scaling for continuous Flow-Matching action experts in Vision-Language-Action autonomous driving. Fine-tuning the $2.28\text{B}$-parameter action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 on $1,600$ curated demonstrations (`Golden-1600`) boosts closed-loop Driving Score by $+11.06\text{ DS}$ over the zero-shot `Base` model ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0076$) and generalizes consistently to $175$ unseen Bench2Drive routes. When scaling to $6,400$ frames, distribution curation (`Golden-6400`) prevents the standstill saturation observed under hazard flooding (`Flooded-6400`), reducing closed-loop stationary time by $-8.19$ percentage points ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0032$) and cutting collapsed short-trajectory predictions by $-67.3\%$ ($p = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$) while maintaining constant per-kilometer crash rates.

**Reproducibility and Code/Data Provenance:** All closed-loop evaluations, telemetry logs (`predicted_trajectories.jsonl`), route-level records (`checkpoint_endpoint.json`), and statistical analysis scripts (`scripts/common/evaluate_confirmatory_plan.py`, random seed $0$ with $20,000$ BCa resamples) are preserved in the workspace repository (`lead` commit `8166a032`, `alpamayo` commit `4cda35d`, `flashdrive` commit `9f6fb43`). Stage-2 Action Expert fine-tuning follows the open-source Alpamayo SFT recipe (`NVlabs/alpamayo-recipes`, seed $42$) [20].
