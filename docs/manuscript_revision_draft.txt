Fine-Tuning Data Composition Sets a Caution–Progress Trade-off in a Vision-Language-Action Driving Policy


Abstract

Vision-language-action driving policies with a flow-matching action expert are adapted by fine-tuning on demonstrations whose composition the practitioner chooses. We show that this choice sets how the policy behaves once it is slow, and that it trades hesitation against progress. We fine-tuned the action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 on a balanced mix and on an obstacle-heavy mix with more stationary samples, drawn from one pool of CARLA expert data and trained on the same schedule with one seed each. Both models were evaluated in closed loop on 44 Bench2Drive routes in two passes, with and without the controller's standstill gate. At 0.5 m/s or less the obstacle-heavy model planned to stay put in 14.00 % of steps and the balanced model in 2.08 %. The difference held without the gate and was reproduced in the second pass (11.74 % vs 1.86 %), as was a test of near-stationary plans fixed before that pass. Neither model planned a stop from motion in more than 0.3 % of steps. On the road the effect depends on the controller. With the gate the balanced model stood still less (registered comparison −8.06 percentage points, 95 % CI [−15.08, −1.25]; second pass −5.09, CI [−11.79, 0.98]); without it total standstill did not differ. The balanced model spent more time commanded to accelerate without moving (8.13 % vs 0.43 % of steps over both passes), and its Driving Score was lower (46.15 vs 51.83). Collision counts did not differ detectably, and we make no safety claim. The mixes differ in three factors at once; [[PENDING: decoupling model, one sentence]]. [[PENDING: H1 on Base-Rerun]].

**Keywords:** vision-language-action models; flow matching; imitation learning; fine-tuning data composition; inertia problem; closed-loop evaluation; autonomous driving


1. Introduction

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for driving combine a pretrained vision-language model (VLM), which encodes camera images, ego-motion history and a navigation instruction, with an action head that outputs a future trajectory. In dual-system designs such as $\pi_0$ [26], $\pi_{0.5}$ [1] and NVIDIA's Alpamayo family [2, 19], the action head is a continuous flow-matching [8] expert that turns a noise sample into a trajectory in a few solver steps. Unlike a regression head, it can represent several distinct futures for one scene and returns one of them at each call.

Such a policy is commonly adapted to a new simulator or vehicle by supervised fine-tuning of the action expert with the VLM frozen. The practitioner then decides what the fine-tuning set contains. A natural instinct is to add more of what is hard: obstacles, hazards, emergency stops. Demonstrations of these scenarios contain more waiting, so the share of low-speed samples in which the expert stays put rises with them; in our data it is 31 % in an obstacle-heavy mix and 11 % in a balanced one. For a policy that samples from the futures it was shown, this share is not a detail. A vehicle that waits when the road is clear blocks the route and fails the task; one that never waits meets the obstacle. Where a policy sits between the two is what a benchmark score and a safety case both depend on.

Imitation-learned driving policies that observe their own motion can learn that a vehicle at rest tends to stay at rest. Codevilla et al. [12] named this the inertia problem; it is a form of causal confusion [13] and of the copycat behaviour described for policies trained on observation histories [25, 28]. The remedies proposed so far act on the model input, the loss or the controller [12, 27, 28]. Outside driving, robot policies have been reported to freeze or idle because of what their training data contain [44, 45], and work on data mixtures and data quality for imitation learning asks which demonstrations raise task success [35, 36]. In driving, adding safety-critical or rare scenarios has been reported to improve collision avoidance and benchmark scores [38, 39], rebalancing has been shown to raise the scores of a flow-matching planner without a language model [33], and Alpamayo 1.5 has been reported to get stuck in CARLA [34].

We have not found a study that changes only the composition of the fine-tuning set of such a policy, holds the model, the loss, the training schedule and the controller fixed, and measures the result in closed loop at the level of the plans the model produces. Without the last step the question cannot be answered, because standstill in closed loop is a joint product of the model and of the controller that executes its plans.

We fine-tuned the 2.28B-parameter action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 on two mixes drawn from one verified pool of CARLA expert samples. The balanced mix (`Golden`) targets 50 % straight driving, symmetric lateral manoeuvres and 2 % stationary samples; the obstacle-heavy mix (`Flooded`) targets a larger share of obstacle and hazard scenario groups, a left-skewed lateral distribution and 10 % stationary samples. Both are deliberate target mixes; neither is uncurated data. Each is built at 1,600 and 6,400 samples, with identical training schedules at each size, and the models are evaluated in closed loop on 44 Bench2Drive routes [3]. We then repeated the evaluation of both 6,400-sample models, ran both with the controller's standstill gate removed, and analysed the logged plan of every step.

The study has three hypotheses. The first two were specified after first results on the same 44 routes were known and before the comparison they concern was evaluated (Section 4.1); the third was specified on 14 November 2026, before the second pass of the obstacle-heavy model existed (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §9).
- **H1 (adaptation):** fine-tuning on 1,600 samples of the balanced mix (`Golden-1600`) raises closed-loop Driving Score over the unadapted policy (`Base`).
- **H2 (standstill time):** at 6,400 samples and a matched training schedule, the balanced-mix model (`Golden-6400`) spends less route time in standstill than the obstacle-heavy-mix model (`Flooded-6400`).
- **H3 (plans, replication):** in the second pass, `Golden-6400` produces near-stationary plans, with a predicted path shorter than 1.5 m, in a smaller share of steps than `Flooded-6400`.

A comparison of the two dataset sizes, called H3 in earlier versions of this manuscript, was exploratory from the start and is reported in Section 4.8 without a number. All other analyses were written into a dated addendum before their values were computed and are labelled post hoc where they are.

We found three things.
- **Data composition shifts what the model plans once it is slow.** At 0.5 m/s or less the obstacle-heavy model planned to stay put in 14.00 % of steps and the balanced model in 2.08 %, on 43 of 44 routes. The difference holds without the gate (10.25 % vs 1.78 %), among steps at rest (16.02 % vs 2.62 %), and in the second pass (11.74 % vs 1.86 %). H3 was met: 3.02 % against 10.13 % of steps (−7.11 percentage points, 95 % CI [−10.82, −4.68]). While moving, neither model planned a stop in more than 0.3 % of steps, so the difference is one of restarting, not of stopping. [[PENDING: motion-history swap test, one sentence]]
- **How that appears in motion depends on the controller.** With the standstill gate, the balanced model stood still less: H2 was met in the first pass (−8.06 pp, CI [−15.08, −1.25]) and not on the second pass alone (−5.09 pp, CI [−11.79, 0.98]); on the mean of both passes the difference is −6.57 pp (CI [−11.18, −1.73]). Without the gate, total standstill did not differ (+2.85 pp, CI [−2.70, 10.32]) and the obstacle-heavy model crawled more (16.93 % vs 12.75 % of steps). Total standstill time is a property of the model and the controller together, and a poor measure of either.
- **Less hesitation has a cost.** The balanced model spent more time commanded to accelerate without moving, always on routes with a collision event (7.75 % vs 0.18 % of steps in the first pass, 8.51 % vs 0.69 % in the second, 14.05 % vs 2.89 % without the gate). Over both passes its Driving Score was lower (46.15 vs 51.83; CI of the difference [−12.10, −0.46]), route completion did not differ detectably, and collision counts did not differ detectably. We make no safety claim in either direction.

The paper contributes (i) a controlled measurement of how fine-tuning data composition alone moves the low-speed behaviour of a flow-matching VLA driving policy; (ii) plan-level measures (stay-put plans by speed, crawling, blocked time) that separate what the model plans from what the controller makes of it, with a closed-loop run without the gate to check them; (iii) the observation that the resulting difference is a trade-off between caution and progress, not an improvement on the benchmark's score; and (iv) an open setup in which a 10B-parameter VLA policy is fine-tuned and evaluated in closed loop on a single 24 GB GPU, with the five datasets released. The quantization method [14], the streaming server [15] and the expert [10] are prior work that we integrate.

The limits are those of the design: one training seed per condition, two mixes that differ in three factors at once ([[PENDING: decoupling model, what it adds]]), one policy, one simulator, 44 routes and a controller tuned on 20 of them. Only H1 and H2 were fixed before their first data; the plan-level findings are post hoc and rest on their replication in the second pass. [[PENDING: H1 on Base-Rerun, one sentence]]

2. Related Work

End-to-end driving. TransFuser [21] fused a single front camera with a LiDAR bird's-eye-view (BEV) representation through transformer attention and reduced infractions in CARLA closed loop. Roach [22] trained a reinforcement-learning expert on privileged BEV input and used it as a coach for a camera-based imitation agent. UniAD [4] chained tracking, mapping, motion forecasting and occupancy prediction into one planning-oriented network, and VAD [5] replaced dense BEV grids with vectorized scene representations. Hydra-MDP [23] distils human and rule-based teachers into a multi-head trajectory decoder that scores candidate trajectories against several evaluation metrics.

Language in the loop. DriveLM [6] structures perception, prediction and planning as graph-structured visual question answering. LMDrive [7] conditions closed-loop control on natural-language instructions. SimLingo [24] is a vision-only VLM driving model that aligns language and actions and reports results on CARLA and Bench2Drive. Alpamayo-R1 [2] and Alpamayo 1.5 [19] combine chain-of-causation reasoning with a flow-matching action expert; we use the latter.

Generative action heads. Diffusion Policy [29] models robot action sequences with conditional denoising diffusion, and DiffusionDrive [30] adapts truncated diffusion with anchor trajectories to end-to-end driving. $\pi_0$ [26] attaches a flow-matching action expert to a pretrained VLM and generates continuous action chunks. Flow matching [8] regresses a velocity field along a probability path; with a linear path and independent noise–data coupling the learned marginal paths are not straight in general, and straightness is obtained only with additional procedures such as minibatch optimal-transport coupling [31] or rectified-flow reflow [32]. These heads can represent several modes; the question we ask is which mode they sample at low speed, which depends on the training data.

Inertia, copycat behaviour and their mitigations. Codevilla et al. [12] documented the inertia problem in behaviour cloning for driving and proposed an auxiliary speed-prediction branch so that the network must estimate speed from images. de Haan et al. [13] framed the broader failure as causal confusion. Wen et al. [25] analysed copycat agents that exploit observation histories and proposed learning representations that suppress the previous action; their keyframe method [28] upweights demonstration frames at expert action changes and was evaluated in CARLA. ChauffeurNet [27] applied dropout to the past-motion input during training so that the model could not rely on it. On the evaluation side, open-loop planning metrics on nuScenes [11] can be matched by models that use only ego status [17, 18], which is the same shortcut seen from a different angle. Many closed-loop CARLA agents also include controller-side anti-stall rules, such as creeping forward after a fixed time at rest; we do not survey them. All of these remedies act on the model input, the loss or the controller. Our intervention acts only on the composition of the fine-tuning set and leaves the model, loss and controller fixed. We did not run a history-dropout baseline or a creep-rule controller, so we cannot say how a data-side change compares with them; Section 5 lists this as a limitation.

Idling, freezing and over-caution. A policy that stops and does not resume is also known outside driving. Kim et al. [44] report that all-zero actions in a robot dataset led a vision-language-action policy to predict such actions and freeze during evaluation, and they removed those actions from the training data. Chen et al. [45] describe policy idling in dexterous manipulation as often a reflection of the training data. In motion planning, the freezing robot problem [42] denotes a planner that judges every forward path through a dense crowd unsafe and stops, and Sadigh et al. [43] observe that planners which only stay out of the way of predicted human trajectories tend toward defensive behaviour. For end-to-end driving in CARLA, Jaeger et al. [40] identify a bias shared by waypoint-predicting models, in which multimodal waypoint predictions are averaged longitudinally and the vehicle slows down, and Chuang et al. [41] address the copycat problem with a residual action target. These works locate the tendency to stop in the data, in the output representation or in the planner. We measure how far the composition of the fine-tuning set moves it for a flow-matching action expert, and what that movement costs.

Composition and quality of imitation data. Which demonstrations a policy is trained on has been studied mainly for its effect on task success. Belkhale et al. [36] characterise the quality of an imitation dataset by action divergence and transition diversity. Hejna et al. [35] learn the weights of a mixture of robot datasets by distributionally robust optimisation and report that the learned weights outperform uniform and hand-chosen ones. Xing et al. [46] trace shortcut learning in generalist robot policies to limited diversity within sub-datasets and to distribution gaps between them. In driving, Hanselmann et al. [38] generate safety-critical scenarios and show that training on them improves collision avoidance, Mirkhani et al. [39] reweight training samples by learned clusters and report closed-loop gains on Bench2Drive, and Zimmerlin et al. [37] find that the style of the expert significantly affects the trained policy and argue against weighting frames by class frequency. These studies ask whether a change of data improves a benchmark score. We did not find in them a report of what a larger share of obstacle and hazard scenarios does to the low-speed behaviour of the policy.

Data balancing and earlier reports on this model. Wang et al. [33] compare sampling strategies for a flow-matching trajectory planner on nuPlan and interPlan and find that reweighting the training data by trajectory pattern is effective. Their planner has no vision-language model, and they report benchmark scores, not standstill. Nguyen et al. [34] evaluate off-the-shelf vision-language-action driving models, including Alpamayo 1.5, in CARLA 0.9.15, moving the vehicle through a model-predictive wrapper instead of a PID controller, and report that Alpamayo 1.5 tends to get stuck on one of their routes. Rebalancing of training data for a flow-matching planner and standstill of this model in CARLA are therefore both on record. What we have not found is a controlled change of the fine-tuning mix for such a model with closed-loop standstill as the endpoint and the controller's part separated.

Closed-loop evaluation. Open-loop displacement metrics hide compounding errors [16, 17, 18]. CARLA [9] executes the policy's commands at every tick; repeated executions of the same scenario in it are not identical, and Chance et al. [47] study this variance and the conditions under which it becomes negligible. Bench2Drive [3] splits closed-loop evaluation into 220 short routes covering 44 scenario types and five abilities (Merging, Overtaking, Emergency Braking, Give Way, Traffic Signs). LEAD [10] reduces learner–expert asymmetries in CARLA expert data; our demonstrations come from the LEAD expert.


3. Methodology


## 3.1 Problem Formulation and Dual-System Architecture

At control step $t$ the policy receives $\mathbf{x}_t = (\mathcal{I}_t, \mathcal{H}_t, c_t)$:
1. **Cameras:** $\mathcal{I}_t$ from four cameras (cross-left, front-wide and cross-right at $120^\circ$ field of view; front-telephoto at $30^\circ$) over a 4-frame window [19].
2. **Ego-motion history:** $\mathcal{H}_t = \{(\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{R}_i)\}_{i=0}^{15}$, 16 poses at $\Delta t = 0.1\text{ s}$ spacing over the last $1.5\text{ s}$ (the last pose is the current one), in the current ego frame ($x$ forward, $y$ left), tokenized into 48 history tokens (three per pose) that form part of the VLM input [2, 19].
3. **Navigation instruction:** $c_t$, a short text such as `Continue straight` or `Turn left in 11m` [19].

The policy outputs $K = 64$ future waypoints at the same $\Delta t = 0.1\text{ s}$ spacing ($6.4\text{ s}$ horizon; the first waypoint is $0.1\text{ s}$ ahead of the current pose), $\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_K]^\top$, $\mathbf{w}_k = (x_k, y_k, z_k)$, in the ego frame at time $t$ [2, 19]. The vertical coordinate is not modelled; it is set to the current ego height.

Alpamayo 1.5 (about 10B parameters) consists of an 8B-parameter VLM backbone $\Phi_{\text{VLM}}$ (`nvidia/Cosmos-Reason2-8B`, Qwen3-VL architecture), which encodes $\mathbf{x}_t$ (the ego history included, as tokens) into a cached key-value state $\mathbf{e}_t$, and a 2.28B-parameter action expert $v_\theta$ that works in a unicycle acceleration–curvature action space [2, 19]. A trajectory is represented by normalized controls $\mathbf{A}_1 = [\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_K]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 2}$, $\mathbf{u}_k = \big((a_k - \mu_a)/\sigma_a,\, (\kappa_k - \mu_\kappa)/\sigma_\kappa\big)$ for acceleration $a_k$ and curvature $\kappa_k$, with $(\mu_a, \sigma_a) = (0.0290, 0.6810)\text{ m/s}^2$ and $(\mu_\kappa, \sigma_\kappa) = (0.000269, 0.0261)\text{ m}^{-1}$. Controls are mapped to waypoints by unicycle integration with trapezoidal quadrature [2, 19], starting at the ego origin with zero heading and at an initial speed $v_0$ that the model estimates from $\mathcal{H}_t$ (it is not a learned output):
$$v_k = v_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k} a_j \Delta t, \quad \psi_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \kappa_j \left(v_{j-1}\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}a_j \Delta t^2\right)$$
$$x_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\big(v_{j-1}\cos\psi_{j-1} + v_j\cos\psi_j\big), \quad y_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\big(v_{j-1}\sin\psi_{j-1} + v_j\sin\psi_j\big)$$
We call $L = \sum_{k=2}^{K} \lVert (x_k, y_k) - (x_{k-1}, y_{k-1}) \rVert_2$ the predicted trajectory extent: the planar path length from the first to the last waypoint (63 segments; the first segment, from the ego position to the first waypoint and about $v_0 \Delta t$ long, is not included, in the controller and in every analysis below). We use $L / 6.4\text{ s}$ as the mean speed the plan implies; over the $6.3\text{ s}$ that the 63 segments cover, the exact mean is $1.6\,\%$ higher. Because the integration starts at $v_0$, $L$ depends on the current speed as well as on the learned controls: with every control at its training mean, a plan from rest has $L = 0.59\text{ m}$, and a plan from $v_0 = 0.4\text{ m/s}$ has $L = 3.1\text{ m}$. Statements about $L$ at low speed therefore condition on speed (Section 4.6). We checked the equations above numerically against the released action-space code (`scripts/paper/check_action_space_equations.py`): on random controls the waypoints agree to within $10^{-5}\text{ m}$.

The action expert is trained by conditional flow matching with a linear interpolation path and independent coupling [8]: $\mathbf{A}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ is drawn independently of the demonstration $\mathbf{A}_1$, $\mathbf{A}_s = (1-s)\mathbf{A}_0 + s\mathbf{A}_1$ for $s \in [0,1]$, and the network regresses the conditional velocity $\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0$:
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1),\, \mathbf{A}_0,\, (\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{A}_1) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \lVert v_\theta(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t) - (\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0) \rVert_2^2 \right]$$
At inference the expert integrates $d\mathbf{A}_s/ds = v_\theta(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t)$ from a noise sample at $s = 0$ to $s = 1$. The expert sees the ego history only through $\mathbf{e}_t$; the history acts a second time through $v_0$ in the integration above.

## 3.2 Why Data Composition Could Change Standstill: A Sampling Hypothesis

The minimiser of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}$ is the marginal velocity field $v^*(\mathbf{A}, s \mid \mathbf{c}) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0 \mid \mathbf{A}_s = \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{c}]$, with $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{e}_t$. Integrating this field from Gaussian noise transports the noise distribution to the training conditional $p_{\text{train}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{c})$, in the limit of exact fit and exact integration [8]. Each inference call therefore returns a sample from the conditional distribution, not its mean. If, in a given context, the training data contain a mixture of futures,
$$p_{\text{train}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{c}) = \sum_{z} \alpha_z(\mathbf{c})\, p_z(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{c}),$$
then a well-fitted expert returns a future from mode $z$ with probability close to $\alpha_z(\mathbf{c})$. Changing the data composition changes the mixture weights, and therefore how often each mode is sampled. It does not shift every sample toward an average. (The previous version of this manuscript argued the opposite; that argument treated the flow as a regressor and is withdrawn.)

The relevant contexts are low-speed ones. When the ego vehicle is at or near rest, the camera image and the ego history are often compatible with two kinds of future: staying put (waiting at a queue, a signal or behind an obstacle) and starting to move. Define, for training samples with ego speed $\le 0.5\text{ m/s}$ at the current frame, three classes of ground-truth future, by the path length $L$ of its $6.4\text{ s}$ trajectory (the quantity defined in Section 3.1): stays put ($L < 0.64\text{ m}$ over $6.4\text{ s}$, i.e. a planned mean speed below $0.1\text{ m/s}$), starts moving ($L \ge 2\text{ m}$), and in between. Our hypothesis is:

**Hypothesis (stay-put sampling).** A flow-matching expert trained on data in which a larger share of low-speed samples stay put will, in low-speed closed-loop contexts, sample stay-put trajectories more often. Because the vehicle then remains at low speed, the next query again sees a low-speed context, so repeated stay-put samples extend the time at rest after the reason for stopping has gone.

The relevant share is the conditional share among low-speed samples, not the global stationary share of the dataset. Table 1 gives both. Among low-speed samples, 31.0 % stay put in the obstacle-heavy 6,400-sample set and 10.9 % in the balanced one; at 1,600 samples the shares are 30.6 % and 11.4 %. The hypothesis predicts that the obstacle-heavy model produces more plans with $L < 0.64\text{ m}$ during standstill, which is what the offline decomposition in Section 4.5 shows. It does not, on its own, predict the 1,600-sample result. There the conditional shares differ almost as much, yet the standstill difference is −1.33 pp with an interval that includes zero (Section 4.8), and standstill with $L < 0.64\text{ m}$ takes a similar share of all logged steps for the two 1,600-sample models (5.17 % vs 5.85 %). The hypothesis is therefore at most part of the explanation. How closely the expert fits the low-speed conditional may depend on the amount of data or on the number of optimizer steps, which differ fourfold between the two sizes. A direct test, sampling many noise seeds in logged low-speed states and counting stay-put samples per model, was not performed.

The lateral asymmetry of the obstacle-heavy mix is a rival explanation for hesitation on obstacle-bypass routes: a model trained mostly on left swerves may be less able to plan a bypass that the route requires. The design bundles both factors, so it cannot separate them (Section 3.3).

## 3.3 Fine-Tuning Datasets

Demonstrations come from the LEAD [10] privileged expert, recorded in CARLA 0.9.16 with the four Alpamayo cameras added and converted to the PhysicalAI-AV layout. All four Matrix datasets are drawn from one verified training pool (`lead_multirig_pai_v4`, 34,132 samples at a 0.4 s stride; 1,840 clips over 646 routes). Nothing was collected separately for either regime. Each sample is labelled with a scenario group (A–F; obstacle avoidance and construction, junctions, hazards and emergencies, highway and merging, vulnerable road users and dynamic conflicts, baseline and signal compliance) and a navigation manoeuvre (straight, swerve left, swerve right, turn left, turn right). A sample is stationary if the ego vehicle moves less than 0.5 m over its 6.4 s future.

Each regime is specified by target marginals: the group shares, the manoeuvre shares and the stationary share. The group × manoeuvre allocation is the minimum-divergence point, an iterative proportional fit of the pool's own group × manoeuvre counts onto the target marginals, integerised with every bucket divisible by four. Within each bucket the stationary target is met as a preference, so it moves no group or manoeuvre marginal. Samples are then selected deterministically, by SHA-1 order salted with seed 0 and spread over as many clips as possible. Each 1,600-sample set is the exact per-bucket first quarter of the 6,400-sample set of the same regime. Brake and hazard shares are outcomes of the draw, not targets. The balanced and obstacle-heavy 6,400-sample sets share 4,284 samples (67 % of each). All cells share one validation split of 1,567 samples.

The two regimes differ in three factors at once (group mix, lateral balance and stationary share), and in this pool the factors are coupled: groups A and C hold 88 % of all left swerves but 58 % of right swerves, so raising the hazard share pushes the lateral balance left. Comparisons between regimes therefore estimate the effect of the bundle. A fifth dataset, `floodedgroups_balanced_6400` (the obstacle-heavy group mix with the balanced manoeuvre mix and 2 % stationary share), would separate the group mix from the lateral-plus-stationary bundle. It has been built and published, and one model was trained on it with the recipe and schedule of the other 6,400-sample models (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §6); its evaluation is [[PENDING: decoupling model, Table 2 row and results]].

The historical obstacle-heavy checkpoint used in earlier versions of this work (`Flooded-6400-Hist`) was trained on a different dataset, `large_v3` (6,440 samples, 10.0 % stationary). That dataset was not drawn by the procedure above, and its training schedule differs (Section 3.4). It appears here only as a sensitivity comparator.

Training and evaluation routes overlap. The LEAD datasets are exempt from the evaluation-overlap filter, so 7.1–7.5 % of each training set's samples lie on a route that overlaps one of the 220 Bench2Drive evaluation routes (lower bounds; 39 log names could not be resolved to a route). Mapped exactly to the 44 evaluation routes we use, training samples in the Matrix sets touch seven routes: 1852 (AccidentTwoWays), 1825 (ConstructionObstacleTwoWays), 2286 (HighwayCutIn), 23901 (InterurbanActorFlow), 23695 (InterurbanAdvancedActorFlow), 3464 (VehicleOpensDoorTwoWays) and 2164 (VehicleTurningRoutePedestrian). The number of touching samples is 20 (golden_1600), 19 (flooded_1600), 78 (golden_6400) and 87 (flooded_6400). Four more of the 44 routes are flagged at pool level but are not touched by any selected sample. The validation split has no overlap. Because the overlap is of similar size in both regimes, it bears mainly on comparisons against `Base`. We report H1 and H2 on all 44 routes and on the 37 routes not touched by training samples (Table 5).

## 3.4 Fine-Tuning, Inference and Controller

Parameter isolation. The VLM backbone is frozen during fine-tuning (`cotrain_vlm = false`, gradients stopped at the trajectory token) [2, 20]. For deployment on one 24 GB GPU the frozen VLM language layers are quantized with pairwise rotation quantization (ParoQuant, W4A8) [14] and served by the FlashDrive streaming server [15]; the action expert runs in `bfloat16`. Only the action expert is trained: 416 parameter tensors, 2,280,592,386 trainable parameters according to the trainer artifacts. Noisy actions and flow time are embedded by `PerWaypointActionInProjV2` (Fourier features, 20 frequencies up to 100) and projected into the 2,048-dimensional expert hidden space [19]. Training follows the Alpamayo Stage-2 recipe [20]: AdamW (fused; $\beta = (0.9, 0.999)$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$, weight decay 0), gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, `bfloat16`, cosine schedule with minimum learning rate from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-6}$, no exponential moving average.

Schedules. Table 2 is rebuilt from the trainer artifacts (`trainer_state.json`, `training_args.bin`, Hydra configurations) and training logs. All four Matrix models used per-device batch 1 with gradient accumulation 8 (effective batch 8), 10 epochs and seed 42. This gives 2,000 optimizer steps at 1,600 samples and 8,000 at 6,400 samples. The configurations of the two 6,400-sample models are identical except for the dataset name, and both trained without interruption. The two 1,600-sample models share a schedule but were each interrupted and resumed from a saved checkpoint. The historical obstacle-heavy checkpoint is a two-phase run: 4,000 steps (stopped at epoch 4.97 of 8 planned), followed by a new 2,415-step run (3 epochs, learning rate $3.5 \times 10^{-5} \to 10^{-6}$, warmup 50) initialised from those weights with a fresh optimizer, scheduler and step counter. The "6,415 steps, 8 epochs" given for it previously are sums, not logged counters. The 20,050 steps previously given for `Golden-6400` belong to a different model (`model_v4`, 16,040 samples), which is not part of this study but was used to tune the controller (see below).

Inference. The FlashDrive server [15] keeps a sliding-window key-value cache and integrates the learned field with $S = 8$ uniform forward Euler steps ($\Delta s = 0.125$, with velocity caching on steps 3–6):
$$\mathbf{A}_{s + \Delta s} = \mathbf{A}_s + \Delta s \cdot v_\theta(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t)$$
Eight steps is the server's configuration, not a choice justified by path straightness; with independent coupling the paths need not be straight [31, 32], and we did not measure the discretisation error. The model server does not seed its noise draws, so each inference call uses fresh noise and closed-loop runs are not reproducible step for step (Section 4.1). CARLA runs synchronously at 20 Hz.

Controller. All runs use the same bridge controller (`alpamayo_bridge_agent.py`, SHA-256 `082c6ecc…10d3`); Figure 1 shows the pipeline. The predicted trajectory is tracked by CARLA's `VehiclePIDController` (lateral $K_P = 1.95, K_I = 0.05, K_D = 0.20$; longitudinal $K_P = 1.0, K_I = 0.05, K_D = 0.0$) towards a target waypoint chosen by a lookahead distance along the trajectory, $\text{clip}(5.5 + 0.3 v, 5.0, 8.5)$ m when the near-horizon turn angle $\phi$ exceeds 0.087 rad and $\text{clip}(5.0 + 0.4 v, 5.0, 12.0)$ m otherwise, with $v$ the current speed in m/s. Here $\phi$ is the largest $\arctan(\lvert y_k \rvert / \max(x_k, 1\text{ m}))$ over waypoints 6 to 35 ($0.6$ to $3.5\text{ s}$ ahead). The target speed follows the trajectory: it is the planned speed to the lookahead waypoint, the path length to that waypoint divided by its time along the plan (for a plan shorter than the lookahead distance this is $L / 6.4\text{ s}$). When the planned speed over the first $2.5\text{ s}$ is lower, the target is $0.6$ times that speed plus $0.4$ times the speed to the lookahead waypoint. It is capped by the stopping-distance limit $3.6\sqrt{2 \cdot 1.8 \cdot L}$ km/h, by 35 km/h and, when $\phi > 0.06$ rad, by $\max(10, 35 - 100\,\phi)$ km/h. A standstill gate sets the target speed and the throttle to zero and the brake to 0.45 ($0.45 + 0.15 v$ above 0.3 m/s, at most 1) whenever
$$(L < 1.5\text{ m}) \;\lor\; (v < 0.5\text{ m/s} \land L < 2.0\text{ m}).$$
The gate keeps no internal state. Its second clause makes it harder to leave rest: below 0.5 m/s a trajectory up to 2.0 m long is still treated as a stop, and the holding brake keeps the vehicle at rest while the clause holds. (We previously called this a hysteretic latch; that described the effect, not a stored state.) When the gate does not fire, the speed is below 1.5 m/s, the target speed exceeds 4 km/h and $L \ge 2.5\text{ m}$, the throttle is raised to at least 0.35 and the brake released (a starting torque). The PID output is limited to throttle 0.6, brake 1.0 and steering 0.8. Steering and pedal commands are smoothed by exponential moving averages ($\alpha_{\text{steer}} = 0.85$ when turning, $0.60$ otherwise; $\alpha_{\text{pedal}} = 0.25$), with throttle and brake mutually exclusive. No controller threshold is configurable.

The PID gains and lookahead were tuned before this comparison on 20 of the 44 evaluation routes (14842, 17563, 1773, 1790, 1825, 1852, 1956, 2050, 2144, 2204, 23695, 2416, 2513, 2534, 2664, 2709, 3364, 3436, 3561, 3936), using `model_v4` (checkpoint-20050). Section 4.4 reports H2 on the 24 routes not used for tuning. The controller and the model therefore act together, and standstill is a property of the pair. Sections 4.5 to 4.7 separate their contributions: offline from the existing logs, and in closed loop with a run without the gate.

4. Experimental Results


## 4.1 Evaluation Protocol and Disclosures

Routes. We use 44 Bench2Drive routes, one per scenario type. With a 10B-parameter policy one route takes about 22 minutes on an RTX 4090, which is why the full 220-route suite was not run for every model. Because of this subset and the custom controller, the Driving Scores reported here are not comparable with published 220-route Bench2Drive results.

Simulator settings. CARLA 0.9.15 (evaluation), synchronous mode at 20 Hz, client timeout 180 s. The launcher sets rendering quality Low on every route outside Town02 and Town03; all 44 routes lie in other towns, so every model ran at Low. (Earlier text stating Epic quality was wrong.)

Termination rule and censoring. The harness ends a route after 4,000 ticks (200 s) or after 1,200 consecutive ticks (60 s) with ego speed below 0.1 m/s. Both are logged as `Failed - TickRuntime`. The standstill fraction is therefore censored: a model that stops for good accrues at most 60 s of final standstill. Routes ended by the 60 s rule / by the tick cap were: `Flooded-6400` 7 / 0, `Golden-6400` 3 / 0, `Flooded-6400-Hist` 3 / 1, `Golden-1600` 5 / 0, `Flooded-1600` 1 / 2, `Base` 0 / 7. The 60 s rule entered the harness on 4 Oct 2026. By result-file dates, 38 of the 44 `Base` routes were evaluated before that (29 Sep–3 Oct), so all seven of its `TickRuntime` routes ran to the 4,000-tick cap. This inflates `Base` standstill relative to the fine-tuned models. `Base` is being re-evaluated under the current rule (`Base-Rerun`), and H1 will use that run: [[PENDING: H1 on Base-Rerun]]. The original `Base` run is kept as a sensitivity row.

Harness changes between the H2 arms. On 10 Nov 2026, between the evaluation of `Golden-6400` (19–20 Oct) and that of `Flooded-6400` (11–12 Nov), the evaluator and launcher were changed: large-map streaming settings restricted to Town12/13, synchronous mode and step set explicitly, guards around scenario shutdown and empty parked-vehicle batches, and the client timeout raised from 60 s to 180 s. We expect these changes to be behaviour-neutral on these routes, but this has not been shown. The second pass ran both models on the current harness: the 44-route standstill means were 28.01 % for `Golden-6400` (first pass 25.71 %) and 33.10 % for `Flooded-6400` (first pass 33.78 %). The harness change did not produce a shift larger than the run-to-run variation reported in Section 4.4.

Non-determinism. The model server does not seed its noise draws. A replicate of route 2534 with the same checkpoint and controller differed from the stored run from the first step (Driving Score 4.1 vs 60.0; standstill 81 % vs 9 %). An earlier claim that repeated evaluations were deterministic was wrong. Per-route comparisons and small strata are therefore descriptive only, and the route-level tests below treat each route's single run as one draw. On eight replicate routes, the standard deviation of the per-route standstill difference between a replicate and the first pass was 16.25 pp (`Golden-6400`) and 35.20 pp (`Flooded-6400`). Between the two full passes it was 22.53 pp and 21.34 pp, with intraclass correlations of 0.57 and 0.63 for standstill, 0.51 and 0.36 for Driving Score and 0.58 and 0.55 for route completion (Section 4.4).

Collisions. The leaderboard merges repeated contacts with the same actor (within 5 s or 5 m) before logging, so logged collisions are not per-tick counts. We additionally merge, within each route, entries with the same actor identifier and report these as collision events next to the logged counts.

Endpoint history and pre-specification. Driving Score, route completion and collisions were analysed first. The standstill fraction (share of logged 20 Hz steps with ego speed below 0.1 m/s) became the H2 endpoint after the Driving Scores of the first 6,400-sample models were seen to be similar. At that time the standstill difference between `Golden-6400` and `Flooded-6400-Hist` was already known. Before `Flooded-6400` was evaluated, we wrote down the endpoint, test, interval and a decision rule for the comparison `Golden-6400` vs `Flooded-6400` and committed them to the project repository on 11 Nov 2026 (commit `013a12bd`). By then the same 44 routes had been evaluated for the other five models, and the endpoint had been chosen from those results. This is therefore a pre-specified replication test on routes that had already been observed for the other models. It is not a registration prior to all data, and the route set was not held out. An analysis addendum, committed on 12 Nov 2026 after the `Flooded-6400` runs finished but before its standstill was computed, fixed the remaining analysis choices, corrected the stratum definitions and pre-specified the controller experiments. Deviations from the protocol were: rendering quality Low instead of the specified Epic; a rerun audit log reconstructed afterwards instead of written during the run; three pre-simulation restarts (route 2534 launched three times and 1852 twice before any simulation step, and one manual watchdog restart); and an incorrect description of the "Open Maneuver" routes. The reconstructed audit shows 45 evaluator launches, 44 results, no route scored twice, and the correct checkpoint served throughout.

Statistics. Route-level differences are paired over the 44 routes. Tests are two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (`scipy.stats.wilcoxon` defaults: zero differences dropped, exact p-value when there are no zeros and n ≤ 50, normal approximation otherwise). We report the statistic $W$, the number of zero differences, the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation $r_{rb}$, and two-sided 95 % BCa bootstrap intervals (20,000 resamples, seed 0). Absolute differences in percentages are given in percentage points (pp). The pre-specified family for Holm correction is {H1, H2}. H1's p-value was known before the family was declared, so the correction offers no protection for H2; we report it as specified. We also report the earlier 4-test family, which included two Driving Score equivalence and non-inferiority tests. All other p-values are unadjusted and labelled as such. Each condition is one trained checkpoint (seed 42), so every test compares two specific checkpoints across routes and says nothing about variation between training runs.

## 4.3 Primary Results

**H2 (standstill time at a matched schedule).** `Golden-6400` spent 25.71 % of logged steps below 0.1 m/s and `Flooded-6400` 33.78 %. The paired difference is **−8.06 pp**, 95 % BCa CI **[−15.08, −1.25]**, Wilcoxon $W = 213$, no zero differences, exact two-sided **p = 0.00071** (asymptotic 0.00100), $r_{rb} = -0.57$; `Golden-6400` had less standstill on 36 of 44 routes. Under the pre-specified rule (A1: interval upper bound below zero and point estimate ≤ −4.0 pp) this is case **A1, replicated**. Holm over {H1, H2} gives 0.0014 for H2 (with H1 from the original `Base` run; to be recomputed with `Base-Rerun`). In the earlier 4-test family (H1; H2; `Golden-6400` vs `Golden-1600` Driving Score equivalence; `Golden-1600` vs `Flooded-6400` Driving Score non-inferiority, both with an 8-point margin), the Holm-adjusted values are 0.0228, 0.0028, 0.0556 and 0.1587. The 8-point margin was not derived from a power calculation or from seed variance and has no formal justification; neither margin test is significant after adjustment. This result compares two checkpoints, one per regime, on one run per route.

Other endpoints for the same pair (unadjusted). Mean speed was higher for `Golden-6400` by 0.31 m/s (CI [0.12, 0.49], p = 0.0003). Route completion was higher by 7.73 pp (82.38 % vs 74.65 %; CI [−0.42, 16.10], p = 0.058; 22 routes tied). **Driving Score was lower** by 3.24 points (49.85 vs 53.09; CI [−13.83, 6.41], $W = 375$, 4 zeros, p = 0.64). Collisions are reported in Section 4.9.

**H1 (adaptation, Driving Score).** [[PENDING: H1 = Golden-1600 vs Base-Rerun, Driving Score, all 44 and 37 non-overlapping routes]]. Against the original `Base` run, which used the earlier termination rule on 38 routes, `Golden-1600` scored +11.06 points higher (49.88 vs 38.82; CI [2.62, 19.71]; $W = 152$, 9 zero differences, asymptotic p = 0.0076; $r_{rb} = 0.52$). The gain is not specific to the balanced mix. `Flooded-1600` gained +6.95 over the same `Base` (CI [−0.86, 14.80], p = 0.064, unadjusted), and `Golden-1600` vs `Flooded-1600` differs by +4.11 points (CI [−5.42, 13.70], p = 0.20, unadjusted). H1 is an adaptation result. It also depends on the routes used to tune the controller. The PID gains and lookahead were tuned on 20 of the 44 routes with a fine-tuned checkpoint (Section 3.4). On the other 24 routes (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §14), `Golden-1600` scored +7.82 points above `Base` (60.80 vs 52.98; CI [−5.41, 21.34], p = 0.20), with route completion +6.18 pp higher (CI [0.88, 15.97], p = 0.21), and `Flooded-1600` scored +1.62 points above `Base` (CI [−9.81, 11.97], p = 0.71). The point estimate keeps its sign, but with 24 routes the interval includes zero, so a gain over the pretrained model on routes the controller was not tuned on is not established by these data. ## 4.5 Standstill by Plan Length, and a Run without the Gate

Offline decomposition. Every standstill step was assigned to one of four bands by the predicted extent $L$ at that step (pre-specified in the analysis addendum):
- $L < 0.64$ m: the plan's own mean speed is below 0.1 m/s, so the model planned to stay put and the gate is not needed to explain the stop;
- $0.64 \le L < 1.5$ m: the gate overrides a planned crawl;
- $1.5 \le L < 2.0$ m with $v < 0.5$ m/s: only the low-speed clause of the gate applies;
- gate inactive.

The band differences sum exactly to the total. For `Golden-6400` minus `Flooded-6400` they are **−7.19 pp** for $L < 0.64$ m (0.75 % vs 7.93 % of steps; CI [−11.26, −4.49]; lower on 41 routes, higher on 2, 1 tie), −3.11 pp for $0.64 \le L < 1.5$ m (CI [−4.72, −1.87]), −0.78 pp for the low-speed clause (CI [−1.30, −0.29]), and **+3.01 pp** with the gate inactive (22.18 % vs 19.17 %; CI [−1.36, 8.53], p = 0.58), all unadjusted. Most of the difference therefore lies in steps where the model itself predicted a trajectory of less than 0.64 m. This is the pattern the stay-put sampling hypothesis of Section 3.2 predicts. In those steps the gate would set the same target speed as the plan does. The gate-active bands together account for a further −3.89 pp, and standstill with the gate inactive is slightly higher for the balanced model. Against the historical checkpoint the $L < 0.64$ m band accounts for −4.78 of the −8.19 pp. The gate was active in 3.73 % of all steps for `Golden-6400` and 15.14 % for `Flooded-6400` (difference −11.41 pp, CI [−17.25, −7.26], p = $6.5 \times 10^{-9}$, unadjusted), and mean predicted extent was 5.75 m longer (30.99 vs 25.24 m; CI [3.77, 7.87]). Figure 3 shows the distribution of $L$ for the two models.

This is accounting, not a causal test. The trajectories were recorded with the gate in the loop, and the gate's holding brake keeps the vehicle at low speed, which shapes the context the model sees next. The decomposition cannot say what the model would have planned without the gate.

[[FIGURE 3: Empirical cumulative distribution of the predicted trajectory extent L over all logged steps of the 44 routes for Golden-6400 and Flooded-6400 (logarithmic axis; values below 0.1 m shown at 0.1 m), with the 0.64, 1.5 and 2.0 m band boundaries.]]

What binds when the gate is inactive. For standstill steps with the gate inactive, we recomputed the controller's target speed before the gate from the logged waypoints (an approximation; the controller uses world coordinates, and the PID output was not re-simulated). For no model was this target below 0.1 m/s. The logged command in these steps was throttle in 69–90 % of them and brake in the rest. `Golden-6400` had fewer such steps with brake (3.21 % vs 5.90 % of all steps; −2.68 pp, CI [−3.87, −1.88]) and more with throttle (18.97 % vs 13.27 %; CI [1.11, 11.76], p = 0.22). Most gate-inactive standstill is therefore not a planned stop: the controller commands throttle while the vehicle is still below 0.1 m/s, for example when starting from rest or, as the blocked-time analysis of Section 4.7 shows, when the vehicle is held in place.

Closed-loop gate-off experiment. To separate the model from the gate, both 6,400-sample models were re-run on all 44 routes with a controller identical to the hashed one except that the forced zero target speed and the holding brake under the gate condition are removed. Speed cap, curve limit, PID gains, lookahead, smoothing and start-assist are unchanged. Endpoints are standstill at 0.1, 0.5 and 1.0 m/s, mean speed, route completion, distance travelled in a common tick window, terminations by the 60 s rule, and collisions by class. The analyses are the Golden − Flooded difference under the gate-off controller and the model × controller interaction (paired difference of differences), both read against the run-to-run spread. The interpretation was fixed in advance. Without the gate the controller still tracks the model's planned speed, so a gap that persists supports a model-side origin, and a gap that shrinks toward the noise floor indicates that the gate amplifies it.

Result (one pass per model and controller; Figure 4). Without the gate, total standstill did not differ detectably between the models: 27.42 % for `Golden-6400` and 24.57 % for `Flooded-6400` (+2.85 pp, CI [−2.70, 10.32], p = 0.17; the balanced model was lower on 29 routes and higher on 15). The model × controller difference of differences was +10.91 pp (CI [2.02, 20.26], p = 0.016). Removing the gate lowered the obstacle-heavy model's standstill by 9.21 pp (33.78 % to 24.57 %; CI [−15.57, −4.35], p = 0.00023) and left the balanced model's unchanged (+1.70 pp; CI [−5.03, 8.75], p = 0.75). At the 0.5 and 1.0 m/s thresholds the gate-off differences were +0.52 pp (CI [−4.84, 7.35]) and −1.33 pp (CI [−6.08, 4.53]). The balanced model still travelled farther in the common tick window (+8.43 m, CI [2.71, 14.84], p = $4.3 \times 10^{-5}$; with the default controller +11.93 m, CI [6.46, 16.58]). Without the gate, route completion was 76.4 % and 81.2 % (−4.72, CI [−14.59, 4.44]) and Driving Score 45.69 and 47.95 (−2.26, CI [−10.41, 5.67]). Both models had 51 collision events, against 44 and 36 with the default controller. The 60 s rule ended 4 balanced and 2 obstacle-heavy routes (3 and 7 with the default controller). As a noise reference, between the two default-controller passes the Golden − Flooded difference changed by +2.97 pp (CI [−6.05, 12.63]) with no change of controller. With the default-controller side taken as the mean of both passes, the interaction is +9.42 pp (CI [2.21, 18.47], p = 0.015). The interaction exceeds the reference, but the reference interval is wide, so the size of the gate's effect on the gap is uncertain. By the reading fixed in advance, the gate amplifies the difference in total standstill: with the gate removed, that difference was not detectable. Sections 4.6 and 4.7 show what lies behind this.

## 4.6 What the Models Plan (Post Hoc)

This section compares the plans themselves, the predicted trajectories logged at every step, and not the resulting motion. All analyses in it were defined after the registered comparison; each was written into the analysis addendum before its values were computed.

Near-stationary plans (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §7a). The share of steps in which the model's predicted trajectory is shorter than 1.5 m, the first clause of the gate, differs between the models under both controllers. With the default controller it was 2.47 % for `Golden-6400` and 13.03 % for `Flooded-6400` (−10.56 pp, CI [−16.02, −6.77], p = $5.7 \times 10^{-10}$; lower on 42 routes). Without the gate it was 2.72 % and 8.94 % (−6.22 pp, CI [−11.27, −3.71], p = $1.5 \times 10^{-7}$; lower on 41 routes). For trajectories shorter than 0.64 m the gate-off shares were 0.65 % and 4.75 % (−4.10 pp, CI [−8.34, −2.35]), and the balanced model was lower on every route. The obstacle-heavy model therefore plans to stay put several times as often as the balanced model whether or not the gate is in the loop. Its own rate is lower without the gate (8.94 % against 13.03 %), which fits the caveat of Section 4.5: the holding brake keeps the vehicle in the low-speed context in which such plans are produced. The two models visit different states in closed loop, so this is not a same-input comparison. The replication test for this share was fixed before any second-pass value existed (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §9, rule R1: difference negative, interval below zero, p < 0.05). In the second pass the shares were 3.02 % and 10.13 % (−7.11 pp, CI [−10.82, −4.68], p = $1.9 \times 10^{-9}$; lower on 41 routes): **replicated**. On the mean of the two passes the difference is −8.84 pp (CI [−12.05, −6.33]; lower on 43 routes).

Plans at low speed (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §10). The stay-put hypothesis of Section 3.2 is about low-speed contexts, so we applied the definitions of Table 1 to the closed-loop logs (Figure 5). Among steps at 0.5 m/s or less, the balanced model's plan stayed within 0.64 m in 2.08 % of steps per route and the obstacle-heavy model's in 14.00 % (−11.92 pp, CI [−16.29, −8.92], p = 1.1 × 10^{-8}; lower on 43 of 44 routes, equal on one). Its plan reached 2 m or more in 88.87 % of those steps against 70.53 % (+18.34 pp, CI [14.39, 23.79]; higher on all 44 routes). Without the gate the shares were 1.78 % against 10.25 % for staying put (−8.47 pp, CI [−12.83, −6.39]; lower on all 44 routes) and 88.97 % against 73.18 % for starting to move (+15.79 pp, CI [12.92, 20.66]; higher on all 44 routes). While the vehicle was moving faster than 0.5 m/s, a plan shorter than 1.5 m was rare for both models (0.06 % against 0.24 % of steps with the gate, 0.06 % against 0.23 % without). The models therefore differ in what they plan once the vehicle is slow, not in how often they plan a stop from motion. In the second pass the stay-put shares at low speed were 1.86 % and 11.74 % (−9.88 pp, CI [−12.64, −7.78]; lower on 42 of 44 routes) and the start-moving shares 88.01 % and 73.88 % (+14.13 pp, CI [10.65, 17.77]). Both first-pass differences have the same sign and intervals that exclude zero, the replication wording fixed in ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §10.

Pooled over all steps of the 44 routes with the default controller, the stay-put share at low speed was 33.3 % for `Base`, 19.8 % for `Flooded-1600`, 16.4 % for `Golden-1600`, 14.6 % for `Flooded-6400-Hist`, 24.9 % for `Flooded-6400` and 2.3 % for `Golden-6400`. The training sets have 31.0 % (obstacle-heavy) and 10.9 % (balanced) at 6,400 samples. The matched pair is ordered as its training sets are, but the shares are not reproduced: the balanced model at 6,400 samples plans to stay put far less often than its own training data do. This comparison is narrower than the one over all steps, but it is still not a same-input comparison, because the two models reach different low-speed situations.

Plans at rest (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §13). The extent $L$ is integrated from the current speed (Section 3.1), so a stay-put share taken over the whole band up to 0.5 m/s could differ between models because they spend different shares of that band near zero speed. Restricting to steps below 0.1 m/s, where $v_0$ is close to zero, the balanced model's plan stayed within 0.64 m in 2.62 % of steps per route and the obstacle-heavy model's in 16.02 % (−13.39 pp, CI [−17.70, −10.36], p = 1.1 × 10^{-8}; lower on 43 of 44 routes). Without the gate the shares were 2.18 % and 12.95 % (−10.77 pp, CI [−15.53, −8.36]; lower on all 44 routes). Between 0.1 and 0.5 m/s, which holds about one in ten of the low-speed steps, stay-put plans were rare in both models (0.19 % and 1.07 %). In the second pass the at-rest shares were 2.38 % and 13.70 % (−11.32 pp, CI [−14.15, −9.03]). The difference of the previous paragraphs is therefore not an artefact of the speed distribution inside the band.

Proceed-cued standstill (keyword heuristic). Classifying each standstill step by keywords in the model's chain-of-causation text, which is not a validated classifier, standstill with a "proceed" rationale (for example "the lane is clear") was 3.21 % of steps for `Golden-6400` and 5.26 % for `Flooded-6400` (−2.05 pp, CI [−7.05, 0.43], p = 0.026, unadjusted). Standstill with a "caution" rationale was 20.97 % vs 28.23 % (−7.26 pp, CI [−14.10, −0.83]). The balanced model has less standstill in both classes, not only in the "proceed" class.

Plans given the stated reason (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §10). The rationale text is produced by the frozen backbone, the same weights in both models. With the keyword classes of the previous paragraph (a heuristic, not a validated classifier), among steps with a "caution" rationale the plan was shorter than 1.5 m in 3.17 % of steps for the balanced model and 14.75 % for the obstacle-heavy model (−11.58 pp, CI [−16.91, −7.82]); among steps with a "proceed" rationale, in 0.83 % against 6.30 % (−5.47 pp, CI [−10.32, −3.05]). Without the gate the shares were 3.26 % against 11.00 % (−7.73 pp, CI [−13.23, −5.10]) and 0.94 % against 4.21 % (−3.27 pp, CI [−8.28, −1.31]). The obstacle-heavy expert sometimes plans to stay when the stated reason is to proceed; the balanced expert rarely plans to stay when the stated reason is to keep distance. In the second pass the differences were −8.30 pp for caution (3.65 % vs 11.96 %; CI [−12.00, −5.90]) and −2.48 pp for proceed (1.57 % vs 4.05 %; CI [−4.49, −1.08]).

Lateral plans (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §10). The two training mixes also differ in their manoeuvre targets (37 % leftward swerves in the obstacle-heavy mix, 13 % in the balanced one), so we asked whether the obstacle-heavy model plans leftward more often. It does not. Among plans of 2 m or more, the share ending at least 1 m to the left was 22.57 % for the balanced model and 16.97 % for the obstacle-heavy model with the gate (+5.60 pp, CI [2.77, 9.66]) and 20.83 % against 20.50 % without it (+0.33 pp, CI [−3.12, 4.78]). The share ending at least 1 m to the right did not differ (29.25 % against 29.43 %; 28.14 % against 28.09 %). We found no sign that the manoeuvre mix explains the difference in contact. In the second pass with the default controller the leftward share was 22.82 % against 19.77 % (+3.05 pp, CI [0.14, 6.67]); as in the first pass the difference is in the direction opposite to the training mix.

## 4.7 What the Vehicle Does: Crawling, Stop Episodes and Blocked Time (Post Hoc)

This section turns from plans to motion: time at crawling speed, the length and ending of stops, and time spent commanded to accelerate without moving.

Time at crawling speed (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §12b). We define crawling as a speed of at least 0.1 m/s and below 1.0 m/s. Without the gate, the obstacle-heavy model spent 16.93 % of route steps crawling and the balanced model 12.75 % (−4.18 pp, CI [−6.48, −2.72], p = 1.4 × 10^{-6}; lower for the balanced model on 37 of 44 routes). With the default controller the shares were 12.39 % and 11.74 % (−0.65 pp, CI [−1.86, 0.73]). The gate sets the target speed to zero when the plan is short, so with the gate the obstacle-heavy model's short plans appear as standstill, and without it part of them appears as crawling. In the second pass with the default controller the shares were 12.63 % and 12.79 % (+0.16 pp, CI [−1.27, 2.17]).

Stop episodes (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §7b). We split standstill into runs of at least 1 s and classed them by length and ending. With the default controller the obstacle-heavy model had more stop episodes per route (5.23 vs 2.27; −2.95, CI [−4.25, −1.59]). By a rule fixed before the episode statistics were computed, the default-controller difference reads as mixed. Stops of under 10 s that the vehicle resumed from, start-up delay and sub-second stops accounted for −4.45 pp (CI [−6.89, −1.80]). Stops of 10 s or more, including those that ended the route, accounted for −3.61 pp (CI [−11.93, 3.82]). Without the gate the first group still favoured the balanced model (−4.78 pp, CI [−8.48, −1.91]) and the second went the other way (+7.50 pp, CI [1.35, 15.95]). In the second pass with the default controller the obstacle-heavy model again had more stop episodes per route (5.36 vs 1.89; −3.48, CI [−4.82, −2.41]); the first group accounted for −6.47 pp (CI [−8.91, −3.48]) and the second for +1.14 pp (CI [−6.71, 8.55]).

Blocked time (post hoc; ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §8). The long stops of the balanced model are mostly not planned stops. From the logged final command, each standstill step is one in which the controller is asking for clear acceleration (throttle at or above 0.35, its own start-assist level, and no brake) or one in which it is not. We call a run of at least 100 consecutive standstill steps (5 s) of the first kind *blocked*: the vehicle is commanded to accelerate and does not move. This is inferred from the command log, not from a contact sensor. The definition was committed after a per-episode listing of long stops had been seen and before any per-route value was computed.

With the default controller, blocked time was 7.75 % of steps for `Golden-6400` (22 runs on 6 routes) and 0.18 % for `Flooded-6400` (2 runs on 1 route), a difference of +7.57 pp (CI [2.84, 16.11], p = 0.028). Without the gate it was 14.05 % (25 runs on 12 routes) and 2.89 % (6 runs on 2 routes), +11.16 pp (CI [5.68, 19.19], p = 0.0022). Every route with blocked time has at least one collision event (6 of 6 and 1 of 1 with the default controller, 12 of 12 and 2 of 2 without the gate). Among the routes without blocked time about half do (20 of 38, 21 of 43, 17 of 32 and 23 of 42). Standstill outside blocked time was lower for the balanced model under both controllers: 17.97 % vs 33.59 % with the gate (−15.63 pp, CI [−24.95, −7.98], p = $7.3 \times 10^{-5}$; lower on 36 routes) and 13.36 % vs 21.68 % without it (−8.31 pp, CI [−13.81, −5.16], p = $7.5 \times 10^{-8}$; lower on 38 routes). Standstill with throttle applied in shorter runs, such as pulling away from rest, did not differ (8.47 % vs 8.97 % with the gate, 6.88 % vs 8.09 % without). With run lengths of 40 or 200 steps, or a throttle threshold of 0.2, the blocked-time difference was between +6.42 and +7.77 pp with the gate and between +9.95 and +11.68 pp without, and the difference in standstill outside blocked time was between −14.48 and −15.84 pp and between −7.10 and −8.83 pp. All of these intervals exclude zero. The replication tests for these two quantities were fixed before the second pass (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §9, rules R2 and R3, Holm-adjusted over the two). In the second pass blocked time was 8.51 % and 0.69 % (+7.83 pp, CI [2.69, 16.14], adjusted p = 0.028) and standstill outside blocked time 19.50 % and 32.41 % (−12.91 pp, CI [−20.99, −7.01], adjusted p = $1.4 \times 10^{-5}$): both **replicated**.

Among the six models with the default controller (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §8a), blocked time was 0.00 % for `Base`, 0.69 % for `Flooded-1600`, 0.83 % for `Golden-1600`, 2.11 % for `Flooded-6400-Hist`, 0.18 % for `Flooded-6400` and 7.75 % for `Golden-6400`. For every model, every route with blocked time has a collision event. `Golden-6400` is the model with the least standstill outside blocked time (17.97 %, against 31.54–40.45 % for the other five) and the most blocked time. Against `Golden-1600` its blocked time was +6.92 pp higher (CI [2.45, 14.58], p = 0.028) and its standstill outside blocked time −13.57 pp lower (CI [−21.74, −7.79]).

The registered endpoint adds the two parts. With the gate the first dominates (−15.63 + 7.57 = −8.06 pp); without it they nearly cancel (−8.31 + 11.16 = +2.85 pp). The gate acts mainly on the obstacle-heavy model's commanded stops: its standstill without a demand for acceleration fell from 24.62 % with the gate to 13.59 % without (−11.03 pp, CI [−17.40, −6.79]). For the balanced model, removing the gate mostly added blocked time (+6.30 pp, CI [1.14, 13.72]).

The two models also fail differently on the same routes. The six routes on which the balanced model has blocked time with the default controller are accident, construction-obstacle, control-loss, side-lane-hazard, parking-exit and pedestrian-crossing scenarios. On them the obstacle-heavy model's standstill without a demand for acceleration was 42.51 % of steps, against 21.80 % on the other 38 routes. Where the balanced model drives into an obstruction and is held there, the obstacle-heavy model tends to stop and hold. Neither solves these scenarios: route completion on the six routes was 64.2 % and 47.5 %. This comparison is descriptive.

The analyses of Sections 4.6 and 4.7 are exploratory and do not change the registered result. They change its reading. The difference between the models in how often they plan to stay put, and in how much standstill the controller commands, is present with and without the gate. The difference in total standstill is present only with the gate, because without it the balanced model's blocked time offsets its shorter commanded stops.

## 4.8 Strata and the 2 × 2 Contrasts (Exploratory, Unadjusted)

Strata. The routes previously called "Open Maneuver" are the 25 Overtaking and Merging routes, renamed "Overtaking + Merging". Contrary to the earlier description, they include signalized and non-signalized junction routes, a bicycle-flow route and five two-way routes with oncoming traffic. On them, `Golden-6400` vs `Flooded-6400` standstill was −10.77 pp (24.7 % vs 35.4 %; CI [−19.58, −5.45]; p = 0.00063). Separately, Overtaking (9 routes) gave −8.28 pp (p = 0.0039) and Merging (16) −12.18 pp (p = 0.034). On Emergency Braking (12 routes) the difference was +0.09 pp (CI [−17.37, 18.97], p = 0.68). On Traffic Signs (5 routes) it was −16.17 pp (p = 0.19), and with the official multi-label assignment (19 routes) −8.67 pp (CI [−21.55, 4.68], p = 0.10). We do not interpret these strata as separating unproductive freezing from legitimate stops: the bridge does not log signal state, actor distances or conflict-zone occupancy, so we cannot tell when stopping was required.

Gap-waiting. On routes with oncoming or crossing flow, waiting for a gap is correct behaviour, so less standstill may partly mean accepting gaps sooner. Splitting the obstacle-bypass routes by oncoming flow (post hoc, descriptive only) gives 13.0 % vs 18.2 % on four single-direction routes and 48.1 % vs 58.9 % on five two-way routes. Both differences favour the balanced model, but with four and five routes and non-deterministic runs they do not separate gap-waiting from freezing. Time-to-pass or gap-acceptance measures would require clearance logs we do not have.

1,600 samples and the 2 × 2 contrasts. At 1,600 samples the regimes differ by −1.33 pp in standstill (CI [−8.26, 6.29], p = 0.11). The difference of differences for standstill, (Golden-6400 − Golden-1600) − (Flooded-6400 − Flooded-1600), is −6.74 pp (CI [−16.60, 2.25], p = 0.079), so an interaction between regime and dataset size is not established. For mean speed the difference of differences is +0.27 m/s (CI [0.03, 0.52], p = 0.017). Dataset size and optimizer steps grow together (2,000 vs 8,000 steps at 10 epochs), so these contrasts cannot separate more data from longer training. With one seed per cell, two sizes do not establish a trend.

**Dataset size (exploratory, `Golden-6400` vs `Golden-1600`; called H3 in earlier versions).** The larger balanced model had less standstill (−6.65 pp, CI [−12.90, −1.74], p = 0.00058), higher mean speed (+0.25 m/s, CI [0.11, 0.40], p = 0.00019), less gate activity (−7.49 pp, p = $2.2 \times 10^{-8}$) and longer predicted extent (+2.76 m, p = 0.00064). Driving Score did not change (−0.02, CI [−8.60, 7.58], p = 0.75), nor did collision events per progress-km (rate ratio 0.99, CI [0.67, 1.46]). Across all 44 routes, the per-route change in route completion and the per-route change in the infraction multiplier are negatively correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.52, p = 0.00033, asymptotic). Since Driving Score is the product of the two, routes on which the larger model gets further also tend to accumulate more infractions. This is consistent with an unchanged Driving Score despite faster progress. A rival reading is equally consistent with these data: the benchmark is correctly reporting that the extra progress was bought with extra infractions. The data do not favour one reading over the other. (An earlier version used a 10-route subset selected after inspection; it is withdrawn.) For the matched 6,400-sample pair the same correlation is weak (ρ = −0.18, p = 0.25).

## 4.9 Collisions and Pedestrian Contacts

Against the matched obstacle-heavy model, the balanced model had **more** collision events: 44 vs 36 (logged entries 46 vs 37). Per progress-km the event rates were 11.07 (CI [7.45, 15.45]) and 9.88 (CI [5.94, 14.92]), a rate ratio of **1.12 (95 % CI 0.80–1.68)**. Per odometer-km the ratio was 1.15 (CI 0.82–1.72), and the per-route event counts did not differ detectably (Wilcoxon p = 0.35). Non-timeout infractions per progress-km had a rate ratio of 0.97 (CI 0.74–1.30). There is no detectable difference in collision rate between the two models. With about 4 km of driving per model, the study is not powered to show that the rates are equal, and no non-inferiority margin was specified. Against the historical checkpoint the event-rate ratio was 0.83 (CI 0.57–1.21), also with no detectable difference. Statements in the earlier version that the balanced model had a constant, reduced or lowest crash rate are withdrawn.

Pedestrian contacts. `Golden-6400` had three logged pedestrian contacts, forming two events. On route 17752 (DynamicObjectCrossing) it struck a pedestrian once; all six models completed this route, and no other model made contact. On route 2164 (VehicleTurningRoutePedestrian) the log has two identical entries for one pedestrian at one position, one event. `Flooded-1600`, `Flooded-6400` and `Flooded-6400-Hist` left that route at 50.3 % completion ("agent deviated from the route"), and `Golden-1600` and `Base` completed it without contact. `Flooded-6400` had one pedestrian contact event, on route 3248 (ParkingCrossingPedestrian), where every model completed the route and no other model made contact. Projecting each route's scenario trigger onto the route polyline (approximate), the trigger lies at 7–9 % of the route on all three routes, so every model reached it. The earlier explanation for route 2164, that the obstacle-heavy models ended before the conflict and were therefore not exposed to it, is not supported by this check. Minimum distances to pedestrians were not logged. Two events against one, on three routes, support no conclusion either way. We report them because they are the only pedestrian contacts among the six models.


5. Discussion and Limitations

Fine-tuning data composition changed what the policy planned once it was slow, and the change was reproduced in a second pass under rules fixed in advance (H3 and rules R2 and R3). What that change does on the road depends on the controller, and it has a cost.

First, the model-level difference holds under both controllers. The obstacle-heavy model produced near-stationary plans several times as often as the balanced one, on 41 or 42 of the 44 routes, with and without the gate and in both passes. This is consistent with the hypothesis that a flow-matching expert samples stay-put futures in low-speed contexts at a rate that tracks their share in the training data, and the training sets differ nearly threefold in that share. It is also the behaviour described as the inertia problem for imitation-learned driving [12]. The difference sits at low speed: once slow, the obstacle-heavy model planned to stay put in 14 % of steps and the balanced model in 2 %, while neither planned a stop from motion in more than 0.3 % of steps. That is the pattern the inertia account predicts. In this action space a plan that stays put at rest is a plan without acceleration (Section 3.1), so the difference can also be read as a difference in how often the model issues a start. Whether the plans depend on the ego history is the subject of a separate test ([[PENDING: motion-history swap test, ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §11-B]]); until it is reported we do not claim that mechanism.

Second, whether those plans become a difference in total standstill depends on the controller. The gate turns a short plan into a held stop, and with the gate removed the total standstill of the two models was no longer distinguishable. With the gate, H2 was met in the first pass and not on the second pass alone (−5.09 pp, interval including zero); on the mean of both passes the difference is −6.57 pp.

Third, total standstill adds two things that moved in opposite directions. Under both controllers the balanced model had less standstill in which the controller was not asking for acceleration, and more time in which it was commanded to accelerate and did not move, always on routes with a collision. Collision counts did not differ detectably over the two passes (Section 4.4), so we do not say that the balanced model collides more. What differs is the time it spends held in place after contact, where the obstacle-heavy model more often stops short. Neither behaviour solves those scenarios.

The reduction in standstill did not translate into a better benchmark result. Against the matched obstacle-heavy model and with the default controller, on the mean of both passes the balanced model had a lower Driving Score (−5.67 points; the interval just excludes zero and the Wilcoxon test does not reject, p = 0.11), a route completion that did not differ detectably, and a collision count that did not differ detectably. The practical reading is narrow. The fine-tuning mix is a lever on how often this class of policy chooses to stay put. In scenarios the policy cannot solve, pulling that lever exchanges waiting for time held against an obstruction. And the standstill fraction should be reported split by command and next to collisions, because on its own it counts a vehicle held against an obstacle the same as one that is waiting.

Limitations.
1. **One training seed per condition.** Each cell is one checkpoint (seed 42), and the 1,600-sample runs were interrupted and resumed. All intervals describe route-to-route variation for these checkpoints, not variation between training runs. The H2 interval upper bound (−1.25 pp) is close to zero. We did not train further seeds.
2. **Bundled treatment.** The regimes differ in group mix, lateral balance and stationary share together, and these are coupled in the pool. The left-skewed lateral distribution is a rival explanation for hesitation on bypass routes. The decoupling dataset `floodedgroups_balanced_6400` exists, but no model has been trained on it; separating lateral balance from the stationary share would need a further dataset.
3. **Endpoint and route set not held out.** The endpoint was chosen after first results, and the pre-specification was committed after the same 44 routes had been observed for the other models. The confirmatory pair was not evaluated on the remaining Bench2Drive routes.
4. **Run-to-run variation and harness changes.** Closed-loop runs are not deterministic, and the two H2 arms were evaluated three weeks apart with harness changes between them. Between two passes of the same checkpoint the per-route standstill differed with a standard deviation of about 22 pp, and the 44-route Driving Score of `Golden-6400` moved by 7.40 points. The second pass gave a smaller standstill difference (−5.09 pp, interval including zero) than the first (−8.06 pp); the plan-level differences were replicated (Sections 4.6 and 4.7).
5. **Train/evaluation overlap.** Seven of the 44 routes are touched by training samples. Without them, the H2 interval includes zero.
6. **Controller.** The PID and lookahead were tuned on 20 of the 44 routes with another checkpoint, a fine-tuned one. This can favour fine-tuned models over `Base`: on the 24 untuned routes the Driving Score gain of H1 is +7.82 with an interval that includes zero (Section 4.3). The standstill gate and holding brake are in the loop for every result in Section 4.3, and the offline decomposition cannot remove them. The gate-off run (Section 4.5) is one pass per model, on routes observed before, with a controller change made after the primary result was known. The plan-rate, stop-episode and blocked-time analyses were defined after that run had started or finished (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §7–8), and blocked time is inferred from the command log, not from a contact sensor. We did not compare against controller-side anti-stall rules or model-side remedies such as history dropout [27] or speed prediction [12].
7. **Censoring and termination.** The 60 s standstill rule censors the standstill fraction. `Base` was mostly evaluated before that rule existed, so H1 awaits the re-run.
8. **Benchmark scope.** A 44-route subset, a custom controller, Low rendering quality and CARLA 0.9.15 at evaluation (data recorded in 0.9.16) make the Driving Scores non-comparable with published 220-route results. Collisions are leaderboard-merged events, and the bridge logs neither signal state nor actor distances.
9. **Streaming and quantization.** Inference uses a W4A8-quantized VLM and streaming key-value cache reuse, whereas training uses full-context BF16. The comparisons are internally valid for this harness; transfer to unquantized or non-streaming inference is untested.

Future work follows from these limits: further seeds for the 6,400-sample pair; a model trained on the decoupling dataset; per-context sampling of many noise seeds at logged low-speed states to measure the stay-put sampling rate directly; evaluation of the matched pair on the remaining routes; logging of contact and of hazards ahead, so that a stop can be classed as required or not; a test that replaces the motion history given to the model at logged low-speed states; and a comparison with history dropout and with a creep-style controller.


6. Conclusion, Availability and Disclosures

We compared two fine-tuning mixes, drawn from the same verified pool, for the flow-matching action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 at two dataset sizes with one seed each. At 6,400 samples and a matched schedule, the mix changed what the policy plans once it is slow: the obstacle-heavy model planned to stay put several times as often as the balanced one, under both controllers and in both passes, and a test of near-stationary plans fixed before the second pass was met (−7.11 pp, CI [−10.82, −4.68]). On the road this appeared as a difference in standstill time only with the controller's standstill gate: the registered comparison was met in the first pass (−8.06 pp, CI [−15.08, −1.25]) and not on the second pass alone (−5.09 pp, CI [−11.79, 0.98]), and without the gate the difference was not detectable (+2.85 pp, CI [−2.70, 10.32]). The balanced model spent more time commanded to accelerate without moving, and it did not have a higher Driving Score or fewer collisions than the matched obstacle-heavy model; we make no safety claim. At 1,600 samples the two mixes did not differ detectably. Fine-tuning itself raised Driving Score over the unadapted policy ([[PENDING: H1 on Base-Rerun]]).

Each dataset card states its composition, construction and evaluation-route overlap. Route-level records, step logs and trainer artifacts are archived at [[PENDING: archive DOI]].

All other analyses in this paper are produced by `scripts/evaluation/paper_reanalysis.py`, which writes a manifest containing every reported number. Both scripts are in the project repository at commit [[PENDING: commit hash]]. The script cited in the previous version (`scripts/common/evaluate_confirmatory_plan.py`) was never committed and is no longer used.
