The Inertia Problem in a Fine-Tuned Vision-Language-Action Driving Policy: Higher Driving Efficiency at a Comparable Driving Score

Abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) driving policies are adapted to new settings by fine-tuning their action head on an intuitively chosen set of expert demonstrations. Imitation-learned policies are known to suffer from the inertia problem: once at rest, they tend to predict staying at rest. Whether the fine-tuning mix alone can create or remove it in a generative policy appears not to have been studied. We fine-tuned the flow-matching action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 once on each of two 6,400-sample mixes from one pool of CARLA expert data: a balanced mix and a hazard-heavy mix with more stationary samples. With model, loss, schedule and controller fixed, we evaluated both checkpoints in closed loop on 44 Bench2Drive routes. In a pre-specified test, the hazard-heavy checkpoint planned a near-stationary path (under 1.5 m in 6.4 s) in 26.80 % of steps, against 1.31 % for the balanced checkpoint and 13.24 % for the unadapted model. The gap recurred in a second pass and on identical replayed inputs, so it lies in the models, not only in the states they reached. The balanced checkpoint stood still about half as long and, in a post hoc analysis, drove faster relative to traffic at a Driving Score that did not differ significantly, but spent more time at rest while commanding motion. A third mix suggests that the effect follows the manoeuvre mix and stationary share, not the scenario types. Each mix was trained once; we do not show that any stop was unwarranted and make no safety claim.

**Keywords:** vision-language-action models; flow matching; imitation learning; fine-tuning data composition; low-speed planning; closed-loop evaluation; autonomous driving


1. Introduction

Vision-language-action (VLA) models are becoming a common design for end-to-end driving. A pretrained vision-language model (VLM) encodes camera images, the recent motion of the vehicle and a navigation instruction, and an action head turns this encoding into a future trajectory [1, 2, 3, 4]. To use such a model in a new simulator or on a new vehicle, the action head is typically fine-tuned on a few thousand expert demonstrations while the vision-language model stays frozen. Which demonstrations enter this set is largely a matter of intuition, and a common heuristic is to enrich it with difficult cases such as obstacles, hazards and emergency stops. How this choice shapes the fine-tuned policy is largely unknown, and one place where it may matter is driving at low speed, where the policy must decide whether to stay stopped or to move off.

Low-speed behaviour is a known weak point of driving policies learned by imitation. Codevilla et al. [5] described the inertia problem: a policy that observes its own speed learns that a vehicle at rest usually stays at rest, and it then fails to restart once the reason for stopping has passed. The inertia problem is an instance of causal confusion [6] and of the copycat behaviour of policies trained on observation histories [7, 8]. The remedies proposed so far act on the model or on its training objective: they add a speed-prediction branch [5], drop out the motion history [9], upweight frames in which the expert changes its action [8] or predict a residual action [10]. None of them changes what the training data contain.

A separate line of work shows that the training data alone can shape what a policy does. For robot policies, the quality, the mixture weights and the diversity of imitation data have been related to the learned behaviour [11, 12, 13]. All-zero actions in a robot dataset led a VLA policy to freeze during evaluation [14], idling in manipulation has been described as often a reflection of the training data [15], and Alpamayo 1.5 has been reported to get stuck in CARLA [16]. In driving, changes of the data have raised benchmark scores through safety-critical scenarios, sample reweighting or a different expert style [17, 18, 19, 20], but these studies report overall scores and not low-speed behaviour. The type of action head gives a further reason to expect the data to matter. Recent VLA policies use a generative flow-matching head [1, 21] rather than a regression head, and in the limit of exact fit and sampling such a head samples each kind of future about as often as the training data contain it in a similar situation. If the fine-tuning set contains many demonstrations that stay put at low speed, the policy may then sample stay-put plans more often, and each such plan keeps the vehicle at low speed for the next decision (Section 3.2).

To our knowledge, these two lines of work have not been brought together. Work on inertia changes the model, and work on data composition measures benchmark scores. It is therefore not known whether the composition of the fine-tuning set alone can create or remove inertia-like behaviour in a generative VLA driving policy. Answering this requires measuring what the model plans and not only how the vehicle moves, because in closed loop a vehicle may be at rest either because the model planned to stop or because the controller turned a short plan into a held stop.

The goal of this study is to answer the following question: when only the fine-tuning mix is changed, does a flow-matching VLA driving policy plan to stay at rest more or less often, and how much of the resulting standstill comes from the model rather than from the controller? We fine-tune the 2.28B-parameter flow-matching action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 [4] on two mixes of 6,400 samples drawn from one pool of CARLA expert demonstrations: a balanced mix, and a hazard-heavy mix with more hazard scenarios, more lateral manoeuvres and more stationary samples. The model, loss, training schedule and controller are the same for both. We evaluate the two checkpoints in closed loop on 44 Bench2Drive routes [22] and measure, at every step, whether the model plans to stay near its position, together with how long the vehicle stands still and how long it is held at rest while the controller commands motion. A second pass, a replay of identical inputs, a run without the controller's standstill gate and a third mix separate the model from the controller and from the states it reached.

Our contributions are fourfold. First, we give, to our knowledge, the first controlled comparison of how the composition of the fine-tuning data relates to the low-speed behaviour of a flow-matching VLA driving policy. Second, we introduce measures at the level of the plan, the motion and the command that separate what the model plans from what the controller makes of it. Third, we show that the fine-tuning mix changes several-fold how often the policy plans to stay at rest, that this difference lies in the models and not only in the states they reached, and that the checkpoint that stays at rest less drives more efficiently relative to traffic (post hoc) at a Driving Score that does not differ significantly, while spending more time at rest with motion commanded; we therefore argue that standstill should be reported split by command and next to collisions. Fourth, we describe a loading check for serving fine-tuned action experts with the FlashDrive inference engine [23], without which part of a fine-tuned expert can stay unloaded without an error (Section 3.4).

Each mix was trained once, so the results compare specific checkpoints and do not establish an effect of the mix across training runs; the logs do not show whether a given stop was required, and we make no safety claim. Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 describes the method, Section 4 reports the evaluation, Section 5 discusses the limitations, and Section 6 concludes.


2. Related Work

End-to-end driving and language. End-to-end planners [24, 25, 26, 27, 28] output a trajectory by regression or by scoring candidates. DriveLM [29], LMDrive [30] and SimLingo [31] connect language with driving. Alpamayo-R1 [3] and Alpamayo 1.5 [4] combine chain-of-causation reasoning with a flow-matching action expert; the latter is used here.

Generative action heads. Diffusion Policy [32] and DiffusionDrive [33] model action sequences by denoising diffusion, and $\pi_0$ [1] attaches a flow-matching action expert to a pretrained VLM. Flow matching [21] regresses a velocity field along a probability path; with independent noise–data coupling the learned paths are not straight in general, and straighter paths need additional procedures [34, 35]. Such heads can represent several modes; the question here is which mode they sample at low speed.

Inertia and copycat behaviour. The inertia problem [5], causal confusion [6], copycat behaviour [7, 8] and the remedies that act on the model [5, 8, 9, 10] are introduced in Section 1. Jaeger et al. [36] note that with waypoint outputs the steering signal is undefined when the vehicle is stopped, which requires additional heuristics in the controller. These remedies act on the model input, the loss, the weighting of training frames or the controller. Our intervention acts only on the composition of the fine-tuning set, and we did not compare it with these remedies (Section 5).

Freezing and over-caution. In motion planning, the freezing robot problem [37, 38] denotes a planner that judges every forward path through a dense crowd unsafe and stops, and planners that only stay out of the way of predicted human trajectories tend toward defensive behaviour [39]. In CARLA, Jaeger et al. [36] identify a bias in which multimodal waypoint predictions are averaged longitudinally and the vehicle slows down. Together with the data-side reports of Section 1 [14, 15], these works locate the tendency to stop in the data, in the output representation or in the planner.

Composition of imitation data and controller-side holds. The studies of data composition introduced in Section 1 [11, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20] ask whether a change of data improves a benchmark score or explains a learned behaviour; none of them takes low-speed planning in closed loop as its endpoint. Que et al. [40] report, for a camera-only planner on Bench2Drive with one seed, that 26 of 42 blocked routes ended with a learned brake output holding the vehicle while the planned trajectory asked for motion; a rule that released this hold reduced the blocked routes from 41 to 24 on the routes common to both runs and raised vehicle collisions from 0.44 to 0.62 per route, without a clear change in Driving Score. That study varies a controller-side rule; we vary the fine-tuning data and remove the hold as an ablation.

Closed-loop evaluation. Errors of an imitation policy compound in closed loop [41], and open-loop planning metrics do not show this: on nuScenes [42] they can be matched by models that use only ego status [43, 44]. Repeated executions of the same scenario in CARLA [45] are not identical [46]. Bench2Drive [22] splits closed-loop evaluation into 220 short routes covering 44 scenario types.


3. Methodology


## 3.1 Problem Formulation and Dual-System Architecture

At control step $t$ the policy receives $\mathbf{x}_t = (\mathcal{I}_t, \mathcal{H}_t, c_t)$:
1. **Cameras:** $\mathcal{I}_t$ from four cameras (cross-left, front-wide and cross-right at $120^\circ$ field of view; front-telephoto at $30^\circ$) over a 4-frame window [4].
2. **Ego-motion history:** $\mathcal{H}_t = \{(\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{R}_i)\}_{i=0}^{15}$, 16 poses at $\Delta t = 0.1\text{ s}$ spacing over the last $1.5\text{ s}$ (the last pose is the current one), in the current ego frame ($x$ forward, $y$ left), tokenized into 48 history tokens (three per pose) that form part of the VLM input [3, 4].
3. **Navigation instruction:** $c_t$, a short text such as `Continue straight` or `Turn left in 11m` [4].

The policy outputs $K = 64$ future waypoints at the same $\Delta t = 0.1\text{ s}$ spacing ($6.4\text{ s}$ horizon; the first waypoint is $0.1\text{ s}$ ahead of the current pose), $\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_K]^\top$, $\mathbf{w}_k = (x_k, y_k, z_k)$, in the ego frame at time $t$ [3, 4]; the vertical coordinate $z_k$ is not modelled.

Alpamayo 1.5 (about 10B parameters) consists of an 8B-parameter VLM backbone $\Phi_{\text{VLM}}$ (`nvidia/Cosmos-Reason2-8B`, Qwen3-VL architecture), which encodes $\mathbf{x}_t$ (the ego history included, as tokens) into a cached key-value state $\mathbf{e}_t$, and a 2.28B-parameter action expert $v_\theta$ that works in a unicycle acceleration–curvature action space [3, 4]. A trajectory is represented by normalized controls $\mathbf{A}_1 = [\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_K]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 2}$, $\mathbf{u}_k = \big((a_k - \mu_a)/\sigma_a,\, (\kappa_k - \mu_\kappa)/\sigma_\kappa\big)$ for acceleration $a_k$ and curvature $\kappa_k$, with $(\mu_a, \sigma_a) = (0.0290, 0.6810)\text{ m/s}^2$ and $(\mu_\kappa, \sigma_\kappa) = (0.000269, 0.0261)\text{ m}^{-1}$. Controls are mapped to waypoints by unicycle integration with trapezoidal quadrature [3, 4], starting at the ego origin with zero heading and at an initial speed $v_0$ that the model estimates from $\mathcal{H}_t$ (it is not a learned output):
$$v_k = v_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k} a_j \Delta t, \quad \psi_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \kappa_j \left(v_{j-1}\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}a_j \Delta t^2\right)$$
$$x_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\big(v_{j-1}\cos\psi_{j-1} + v_j\cos\psi_j\big), \quad y_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\big(v_{j-1}\sin\psi_{j-1} + v_j\sin\psi_j\big)$$
The quantity $L = \sum_{k=2}^{K} \lVert (x_k, y_k) - (x_{k-1}, y_{k-1}) \rVert_2$ is called the predicted trajectory extent: the planar path length from the first to the last waypoint (63 segments; the segment from the ego position to the first waypoint is not included). Because the integration starts at $v_0$, $L$ depends on the current speed as well as on the learned controls: with every control at its training mean, a plan from rest has $L = 0.59\text{ m}$, and a plan from $v_0 = 0.4\text{ m/s}$ has $L = 3.1\text{ m}$. Statements about $L$ at low speed therefore condition on speed (Section 4.3). Supplementary Section S3.1 gives a numerical check of these equations.

The action expert is trained by conditional flow matching with a linear interpolation path and independent coupling [21]: $\mathbf{A}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ is drawn independently of the demonstration $\mathbf{A}_1$, $\mathbf{A}_s = (1-s)\mathbf{A}_0 + s\mathbf{A}_1$ for $s \in [0,1]$, and the network regresses the conditional velocity $\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0$:
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim p(s),\, \mathbf{A}_0,\, (\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{A}_1) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \lVert v_\theta(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t) - (\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0) \rVert_2^2 \right]$$
Here $p(s)$ is the flow-time distribution of the training recipe [47], which was not changed ([3] describes a shifted beta distribution; the distribution in the recipe version used here was not verified). At inference the expert integrates $d\mathbf{A}_s/ds = v_\theta(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t)$ from a noise sample at $s = 0$ to $s = 1$. The expert sees the ego history only through $\mathbf{e}_t$; the history acts a second time through $v_0$ in the integration above.

## 3.2 Why Data Composition Could Change Low-Speed Plans: A Sampling Conjecture

The minimiser of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}$ is the marginal velocity field, and integrating it from Gaussian noise transports the noise distribution to the training conditional $p_{\text{train}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{e}_t)$ in the limit of exact fit and exact integration [21]. In that limit each inference call returns a sample from the conditional distribution, not its mean, so a well-fitted expert returns each kind of future about as often as the training data contain it in a given context. The deployed sampler is far from the limit: it evaluates the expert four times per call, and a coarse integration can pull samples toward the conditional mean, a rival mechanism for short plans that we did not test.

The relevant contexts are low-speed ones, where the camera image and the ego history are often compatible both with staying put and with starting to move. For samples with ego speed $\le 0.5\text{ m/s}$ three classes of future are defined by the path length $L$ of the $6.4\text{ s}$ trajectory: stays put ($L < 0.64\text{ m}$, a planned mean speed below $0.1\text{ m/s}$), starts moving ($L \ge 2\text{ m}$), and in between.

**Conjecture (stay-put sampling).** A flow-matching expert trained on data in which a larger share of low-speed samples stay put will, in low-speed closed-loop contexts, sample stay-put trajectories more often. Because the vehicle then remains at low speed, the next query again sees a low-speed context, so repeated stay-put samples extend the time at rest after the reason for stopping has gone.

The conjecture is not one of our tests. It was formulated after first closed-loop results of these checkpoints were known, so their agreement with it is not a test of it. The relevant share is the conditional one among low-speed samples (Table 1): 30.9 % of them stay put in the hazard-heavy set and 10.9 % in the balanced one. The conjecture does not account for the balanced checkpoint planning to stay put far less often than its training share (Tables 1 and 3; Section 5), so it is at most part of the explanation. Supplementary Section S3.2 gives the argument in full, with a further condition that we did not measure and the rival explanations that the design cannot separate.

## 3.3 Fine-Tuning Datasets

Demonstrations come from the LEAD [48] privileged expert, recorded in CARLA 0.9.16 at rendering quality Epic. The two fine-tuning sets of the main design, one per mix and each of 6,400 samples, are drawn from one verified training pool of 34,132 samples. Each sample is labelled with a scenario group (A–F) and a navigation manoeuvre. A sample is stationary if the ego vehicle moves less than 0.5 m over its 6.4 s future.

Each mix, or regime, is specified by target marginals: the group shares, the manoeuvre shares and the stationary share (Table 1). The balanced and hazard-heavy sets share 4,284 samples (66.9 % of each). All sets share one validation split of 1,567 samples.

The two regimes differ in three factors at once (group mix, lateral balance and stationary share), and in this pool the factors are coupled, so comparisons between regimes estimate the effect of the bundle. A third set of 6,400 samples has the scenario-group shares of the hazard-heavy mix with the manoeuvre shares and the 2 % stationary share of the balanced mix. It is called the decoupling mix here; it separates the group mix from the lateral-plus-stationary bundle. We trained one model, `Decoupling-6400`, on it with the recipe and schedule of the other two models (Section 4.5).

Training and evaluation routes overlap: training samples of the hazard-heavy and balanced sets touch seven of the 44 evaluation routes (87 and 78 samples); the count for the decoupling set, which shares 86.3 % of its samples with the balanced set, was not determined. Because the overlap is of similar size in both regimes, it bears mainly on comparisons against the unadapted Alpamayo 1.5, which is called `Base` here. Section 4.3 reports the two main contrasts on the 37 untouched routes (values in Supplementary Section S4.4), and Supplementary Section S3.3 gives the scenario groups, the construction and the overlap in full.

## 3.4 Fine-Tuning, Inference and Controller

Parameter isolation. The VLM backbone is frozen during fine-tuning [3, 47]; only the action expert is trained, with the Alpamayo Stage-2 recipe [47]. For deployment on one 24 GB GPU the frozen VLM language layers are quantized to 4-bit weights with pairwise rotation quantization (ParoQuant) [49] and run with 8-bit activations in our model server, which is built on the FlashDrive inference engine [23]; the action expert runs in `bfloat16`.

Schedules. The three fine-tuned models used an effective batch of 8, 10 epochs and seed 42, which gives 8,000 optimizer steps. The balanced and hazard-heavy models have identical configurations except for the dataset and trained without interruption (Supplementary Table S1).

Inference. The model server, built on the FlashDrive engine [23], keeps a sliding-window key-value cache and integrates the learned field in eight uniform forward-Euler steps. Our server code reuses the velocity on four of the eight steps, so the expert is evaluated four times per call; we did not measure the discretisation error. The server does not seed its noise draws, so closed-loop runs are not reproducible step for step (Section 4.1). CARLA runs synchronously at 20 Hz, and at every step the agent queries the server and acts on the plan it returns.

Loading of the fine-tuned expert. The FlashDrive release contains the inference engine and no server for fine-tuned experts; the serving script is our own. A serving script that first loads the base model and then copies the tensors of a fine-tuned action expert into it by name, skipping names that find no match, does not load the whole expert: because the engine merges projections of each expert layer, 180 of the 396 expert-layer tensors would keep their base values, without a message (Supplementary Section S3.4). Every fine-tuned checkpoint is therefore served with a full-loading server. It refuses to start unless all 416 tensors of the checkpoint file are found in memory with a largest difference of 0, and stores a report of this check with every route. `Base` needs no such step.

Controller. All runs except those of Sections 4.4 and 4.7 use the same bridge controller (Figure 1). CARLA's `VehiclePIDController` tracks the predicted trajectory towards a lookahead waypoint, with a target speed that follows the planned speed up to 35 km/h. The target speed, the gate and every extent-based measure in this paper are read from the predicted waypoints, whose speed information Jaeger et al. [36] found to be ambiguous for multimodal plans. A standstill gate sets the target speed and the throttle to zero and applies the brake whenever
$$(L < 1.5\text{ m}) \;\lor\; (v < 0.5\text{ m/s} \land L < 2.0\text{ m}).$$
The gate keeps no internal state, and its second clause makes it harder to leave rest. When the gate does not fire, the speed is below 1.5 m/s, the target speed exceeds 4 km/h and $L \ge 2.5\text{ m}$, the throttle is raised to at least 0.35 and the brake released (a starting torque). Supplementary Section S3.4 gives the controller in full.

We tuned the proportional-integral-derivative (PID) gains and lookahead before this comparison on 20 of the 44 evaluation routes, with a fine-tuned checkpoint that is not part of this study; Section 4.3 reports the two main contrasts on the other 24 routes (values in Supplementary Section S4.4). Standstill is a property of controller and model together, and Section 4.4 separates their contributions with a run without the gate.

4. Experimental Results


## 4.1 Evaluation Protocol and Disclosures

Routes and simulator. The evaluation uses 44 Bench2Drive routes, one per scenario type; at about 22 minutes per route, we did not run the full 220-route suite for every model. Because of this subset and the custom controller, the Driving Scores reported here are not comparable with published 220-route Bench2Drive results. Evaluation ran in CARLA 0.9.15, in synchronous mode at 20 Hz and at rendering quality Low.

Runs and stages. We served every fine-tuned checkpoint with the full-loading server of Section 3.4. All runs after the first pass of the two checkpoints of the main pair formed a second stage, which was to follow if the plan-level test of the first pass was met, as it was. One server process served all routes of a job, and its streaming cache was not cleared between routes; this is the same for every checkpoint and pass.

Termination rule and censoring. The harness ends a route after 4,000 ticks (200 s) or after 1,200 consecutive ticks (60 s) with ego speed below 0.1 m/s. The standstill fraction is therefore censored: a model that stops for good accrues at most 60 s of final standstill. We evaluated the unadapted policy under the same rule. This run is called `Base-Rerun`, because a preliminary run of `Base` predates the rule, and every closed-loop comparison with the unadapted policy uses it.

Non-determinism. The model server does not seed its noise draws, so two runs of the same checkpoint differ from the first step. Between the two passes of each checkpoint of the main pair the per-route standstill difference had a standard deviation of 24.73 pp (`Balanced-6400`) and 18.29 pp (`Hazard-heavy-6400`) (post hoc). Per-route comparisons and small strata are therefore descriptive only.

Collisions. The leaderboard merges repeated contacts with the same actor before logging; in our analysis, we additionally merge entries with the same actor identifier within each route and report them as collision events.

Pre-specification. (i) The tests of the first pass (F1, F2) and the repeat rule for the second pass (R1 to R3), all defined in Section 4.3, were fixed in writing before the first route of the evaluation was run. We had used the checkpoints, the routes and the measures in preliminary evaluations before that, so these are pre-specified tests on new runs, not a registration prior to all data. (ii) The readings of four further analyses were also fixed before their data: the same-input replay, the decoupling checkpoint, the run without the gate and the history-swap test. We had run each of these four analyses once in the preliminary evaluations and knew its outcome before the runs reported in this paper; we did not change the rules. (iii) All other analyses are post hoc or exploratory and are labelled as such. We changed no decision rule after any look at incomplete data. We recorded the pre-specified rules of items (i) and (ii) in writing, each with its date, before the runs to which they apply. The dates were not time-stamped by a third party; Supplementary Section S4.1 gives the full record with its dates.

Statistics. Route-level differences are paired over the 44 routes, or over the number of routes given with a result. Tests are two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and intervals are two-sided 95 % bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap intervals for the mean paired difference. A rank test and an interval for the mean can disagree, and we state this where they do. For blocked time, which is zero on most routes, an exact sign-flip permutation test of the mean difference is also given. Differences in percentages are given in percentage points (pp). We apply Holm adjustment over the two tests named with each rule; all other p-values are unadjusted. Each condition is one trained checkpoint (seed 42), so every test compares two specific checkpoints across routes and says nothing about variation between training runs. Table 2 defines the measures, and Supplementary Section S4.1 gives further detail.

## 4.2 Same-Input Replay

Closed-loop rates of plans are taken in the states each model reached, so they do not compare the experts on the same inputs. We therefore replayed recorded states through the engine of the model server. The states are the 384 samples of the shared validation split with a speed of 0.5 m/s or less, from 31 source logs; the split is held out by route from every training set. For each state the engine returned 16 sampled plans, with a random seed fixed per state and the same for every expert. The outcome is the stay-put share, the share of the 16 plans with $L < 0.64$ m, averaged within each source log and compared over the 31 logs. We fixed the comparison in writing before any inference of a preliminary replay. We fixed the statistic reported here and its reading after that preliminary replay, with its outcome known, and before any of the runs of this section. In the demonstrations the future stays put in 15 of the 384 states (3.91 %).

Do the experts differ on identical inputs? They do. `Balanced-6400` had a stay-put share of 0.35 % and `Hazard-heavy-6400` of 17.61 %, as means over the 31 source logs (**−17.26 pp**, CI [−23.78, −12.13], p = $5.6 \times 10^{-6}$). Pooled over the 384 states the shares are 0.63 % and 22.71 %. The rule fixed in advance reads a negative difference with an interval below zero as a difference between the two experts on identical inputs, for low-speed states reached by the demonstrator, and an interval that includes zero as no shown difference. By this rule the two experts differ on identical inputs.

In which direction did fine-tuning move the policy? In both, in these two checkpoints (descriptive). `Base` returned stay-put plans in 3.28 %. Against it, `Balanced-6400` was lower (−2.92 pp, CI [−4.60, −1.95]) and `Hazard-heavy-6400` higher (+14.33 pp, CI [9.46, 20.62]). On these states, the one training run on the balanced mix therefore lowered the stay-put share of the base model and the one on the hazard-heavy mix raised it; with one run per mix this shows the direction of each checkpoint and not an effect of the mix. Supplementary Section S4.3 gives the decoupling checkpoint.

What do the plans look like on a single state? Figure 2 shows the 16 plans of the three fine-tuned checkpoints on two replayed states, chosen by a rule fixed before any state was looked at. It is an illustration without a test. Panel (b) is the extreme of the set, not a typical state, and which plan is right is not known for either state.

Is the replay reproducible? Within a process it is; between processes, not fully. A later process of the unchanged base model had a stay-put share of 4.23 % against 3.28 % for the first, and the cause is not known. The comparisons of this section therefore carry an uncertainty of about 1 pp, estimated from one pair of processes of one model. It is small against the difference between the two checkpoints of the main pair and not small against the smaller contrast of Supplementary Section S4.3 (`Decoupling-6400` against `Balanced-6400`), which is reported but not relied on. The contrast between `Balanced-6400` and `Base` holds against the later base process as well (−3.88 pp, CI [−5.74, −2.59]). Supplementary Section S4.3 also gives the full protocol and all checks.

What does the replay not show? The states are those a privileged expert driver reached; states that only the learned policies reach, such as those after a collision, are absent. We replayed each state as two consecutive frame windows, as at the start of streaming deployment (Supplementary Section S4.3), not as a long stream. The replay shows that the experts differ on the same inputs. It does not show why, and with one training run per mix it cannot attribute the difference to the data mix.


## 4.3 The Matched Pair

Protocol and tests fixed before the runs. We evaluated the two checkpoints of the main pair on the 44 routes in a first and a second pass, and all routes were scored and counted (Supplementary Section S4.4). F1, the primary test, is the share of logged steps with a near-stationary plan ($L < 1.5$ m), `Balanced-6400` minus `Hazard-heavy-6400`, paired by route. It is met if the difference is negative, the interval lies below zero and p < 0.05. F2 is the standstill time, read by cases fixed in advance: an interval below zero with a point estimate of −4.0 pp or lower counts as a strong result. Both are Holm-adjusted over the two. By the repeat rule, decided on the second pass alone, the share of near-stationary plans (R1), the standstill outside blocked time (R2) and the blocked time (R3) each count as replicated if the difference has the sign of the first pass, its interval excludes zero and p < 0.05, with Holm adjustment over R2 and R3. All other comparisons in this section are descriptive.

Does the balanced checkpoint return near-stationary plans less often? It does, by a wide margin (Table 3; Figure 3 shows both passes pooled). `Balanced-6400` returned a near-stationary plan in 1.31 % of steps and `Hazard-heavy-6400` in 26.80 %, a difference of **−25.49 pp** (CI [−31.69, −19.63], p = $1.1 \times 10^{-13}$, Holm $2.3 \times 10^{-13}$; **F1 is met**). Among the steps at 0.5 m/s or less the difference in stay-put plans was of the same size (Table 3), in the direction of the shares on identical inputs (Section 4.2).

Does the vehicle stand still less? It does. `Balanced-6400` stood still for 22.97 % of the route time and `Hazard-heavy-6400` for 46.66 %, a difference of **−23.69 pp** (CI [−33.48, −15.08], p = $3.3 \times 10^{-6}$, Holm $3.3 \times 10^{-6}$; **F2 is a strong result** by the rule fixed in advance). `Hazard-heavy-6400` also spent more time crawling and drove at less than half the mean speed (Table 3).

Is the standstill of the two checkpoints of the same kind? It is not. `Hazard-heavy-6400` had no blocked time on any route: none of its standstill was time in which the controller commanded acceleration for 5 s or more and the vehicle did not move. `Balanced-6400` had blocked time on 10 routes (12.69 % of steps on average; sign-flip permutation test p = 0.002). Outside blocked time it stood still for 10.28 % of steps against 46.66 % (−36.38 pp, CI [−45.55, −28.16]). Part of the standstill that the balanced checkpoint kept was thus time in which it was commanded to move and did not.

What is the vehicle doing while it is blocked? It keeps commanding forward motion, in most cases within 10 m of a recorded collision position (post hoc, descriptive). The plans at the blocked steps of `Balanced-6400` were forward plans: 2.0 m or longer in 97.7 % of them in the first pass and 99.7 % in the second. Of these steps, 91.4 % and 100.0 % lay within 10 m of a recorded collision position, a match that is weak evidence taken alone and not specific to blocked time. The records give neither the order of contact and stop nor the duration of contact. Blocked time is therefore co-located with recorded collisions, and whether it adds harm beyond the recorded contact cannot be measured from these logs (Supplementary Sections S4.2 and S4.5; Table S2). Blocked time is not a second form of staying put: the model asks for motion, so a controller rule that releases a held stop would not remove it.

Does the balanced checkpoint score higher on the benchmark? Not detectably. In the first pass `Balanced-6400` had the higher Driving Score (+10.52 points, CI [2.59, 19.09], p = 0.044). One limit applies to all of this section: two passes address evaluation noise, of which a difference between server processes (Section 4.2) is a part, and they do not address variation between training runs, because each mix has one checkpoint.


## 4.4 The Matched Pair without the Standstill Gate

Protocol. We ran both checkpoints of the main pair once on the 44 routes with a controller that is identical to the default one except that the forced zero target speed and the holding brake under the gate condition are removed. We fixed the reading in advance: a difference that persists without the gate supports an origin in the plans, and a difference that shrinks toward the noise reference, the difference between the two default passes, indicates that the gate amplifies it. The comparator with the gate is the per-route mean of the two default passes (Table 4). The analysis is exploratory, with one pass per checkpoint.

Does the difference in full stops, that is, in standstill time below 0.1 m/s, need the gate? Largely (Table 5, Figure 5). Without the gate `Balanced-6400` stood still for 18.76 % of the route time and `Hazard-heavy-6400` for 23.41 % (−4.65 pp, CI [−11.44, 3.20]); with the gate the difference was −23.54 pp. The signed-rank test rejects for the gate-off difference (p = 0.0048) while the interval of its mean includes zero, so a smaller difference in the same direction is not excluded. Removing the gate lowered the standstill of `Hazard-heavy-6400` by 24.14 pp and that of `Balanced-6400` by 5.24 pp (Table 5), a difference of +18.89 pp between the two changes (CI [11.29, 27.16]). The same quantity between the two default passes, with no change of controller, was +0.30 pp (CI [−8.75, 9.62]). By the reading fixed in advance, the gate amplifies the difference in full stops.

What persists without the gate? The difference in the plans and in slow driving. `Balanced-6400` returned a near-stationary plan in 0.60 % of steps and `Hazard-heavy-6400` in 14.58 % (−13.99 pp, CI [−20.27, −9.52]). The time below 1.0 m/s differed by −21.40 pp (CI [−28.67, −12.77]), because `Hazard-heavy-6400` now crawled where it had stood still. Blocked time stayed with `Balanced-6400` (12.62 % against 0.35 %; +12.27 pp, CI [5.14, 22.77]). By the same reading, these differences support an origin in the plans. The rate of near-stationary plans is not a property of the model alone. Without the gate `Hazard-heavy-6400`, with the same weights, returned such plans about half as often as with it (−13.46 pp, CI [−19.34, −8.65]). The interpretation offered here is a feedback between gate and plans, which this run does not test directly (Supplementary Section S4.7).

What did removing the gate do to the outcomes? For `Hazard-heavy-6400` it exchanged waiting for progress and collisions at an unchanged score. Its route completion rose (+10.95 pp, CI [3.97, 18.02]), its collision events per route rose (+0.42, CI [0.14, 0.72]), and its Driving Score did not change detectably (−1.87 points, CI [−10.16, 5.41]). ## 4.5 The Decoupling Checkpoint

Does the scenario mix or the manoeuvre mix carry the difference? `Decoupling-6400` has the scenario-group mix of the hazard-heavy set and the manoeuvre mix and stationary share of the balanced set (Section 3.3). We fixed its readings, from one pass of the 44 routes, before the checkpoint had run any route and apply them unchanged, with the per-route mean of the two passes of each of the other two checkpoints as comparator. We fixed two readings. The plan-level reading compares the share of near-stationary plans of `Decoupling-6400` with that of each of the other two checkpoints: if it is lower than that of the hazard-heavy checkpoint with an interval below zero, and its difference from the balanced checkpoint has an interval that includes zero, near-stationary planning is read as going with the manoeuvre mix and the stationary share and not with the scenario-group mix. By the remedy reading, the mix may be called a candidate remedy only if it has fewer near-stationary plans than the hazard-heavy checkpoint, no more blocked time and no lower Driving Score, each judged by its interval. How far does this reading go? ## 4.6 The Fine-Tuned Checkpoints against the Unadapted Policy (Post Hoc)

Did fine-tuning move the policy toward staying put or away from it? In both directions in these checkpoints. We added this comparison after the first pass was known; it has no decision rule, and the unadapted policy has one pass (`Base-Rerun`, Section 4.1), so its own pass-to-pass variation is not averaged out. `Base-Rerun` returned a near-stationary plan in 13.24 % of steps. `Balanced-6400` lay below it (1.22 % over both passes; −12.02 pp, CI [−18.04, −7.75]) and `Hazard-heavy-6400` above it (28.04 %; +14.80 pp, CI [7.95, 21.62]), as on identical inputs (Section 4.2). Standstill time followed: −12.27 pp for `Balanced-6400` (CI [−21.55, −3.85]) and +11.27 pp for `Hazard-heavy-6400` (CI [1.00, 20.86]), against 36.28 % for `Base-Rerun`.

Did fine-tuning raise the benchmark score? For `Balanced-6400` the mean score was higher, and the two statistics disagree. Against the unadapted policy its mean Driving Score over both passes was higher by 8.92 points (51.52 against 42.60; 95 % CI [0.96, 18.04]); the score was higher on 25 routes, lower on 14 and equal on 5. The interval of the mean excludes zero, and the rank test does not reject (p = 0.058). The unadapted policy has one pass and the comparison is post hoc. For `Hazard-heavy-6400` and `Decoupling-6400` the interval of the difference includes zero (means in Table 6; intervals in Supplementary Section S4.9).


## 4.7 Motion-History Swap Test (Exploratory)

Protocol. The usual account of the inertia problem is that a stay-put plan follows the policy's own speed or recent motion and not the scene [5, 7, 9]. In this exploratory test we replaced the ego history of Section 3.1, the only non-visual motion input of the model, on about one step in five and read the plan that the model returned, without acting on it. On a step at 0.5 m/s or less the history was replaced by a *moving history*, a straight line behind the vehicle at a constant 4.0 m/s; the camera frames and the navigation command were unchanged. We ran `Hazard-heavy-6400` and `Balanced-6400` once each on eight routes. We fixed the outcomes, their reading and the limits below before any run with a replaced history existed and did not change them afterwards; a preliminary evaluation with this test preceded the runs of this section.

Outcomes. A replaced history changes the extent $L$ mechanically (Section 3.1), so the outcomes are defined on the planned change of speed over the plan, which depends on the learned accelerations only. A plan holds speed if its planned speed changes by less than 0.5 m/s over the plan, accelerates if it gains 0.5 m/s or more, decelerates if it loses 0.5 m/s or more, and ends at rest if its final speed is below 0.5 m/s (Supplementary Section S4.10 gives the exact definitions). Among steps at 0.5 m/s or less, W-a (primary) is the share of plans that hold speed under the moving history minus that share under the true history, and W-b is the same difference for plans that end at rest. A checkpoint with fewer than five routes of at least 20 steps in both conditions receives no reading. By the rule, the learned controls *depend on the ego history* if the interval of W-a excludes zero. The stay-put plans may be tied to a history that reports rest only if, in addition, the share of plans that hold speed is lower under the moving history.

Do the controls of the hazard-heavy checkpoint depend on the ego history? They do, and not in the direction that this rule names. On the six routes that count, `Hazard-heavy-6400` planned to hold its speed in 84.29 % of the steps with the moving history and in 52.29 % of the steps with the true history (W-a: +32.00 pp, CI [22.63, 36.04], Wilcoxon p = 0.031, the smallest value possible with six routes). It planned to accelerate in 15.71 % against 47.65 %. The interval of W-a excludes zero, so the learned controls depend on the ego history. The share of plans that hold speed rose under the moving history and did not fall, so the condition for tying the stay-put plans to a history that reports rest is not met, and we report the outcome as found: told that it was moving at 4.0 m/s, this checkpoint more often planned to keep that speed and less often planned to accelerate.

Are the stay-put plans matched by braking plans? They are not. With the true history, 44.76 % of the plans of `Hazard-heavy-6400` at low speed ended at rest; with the moving history none did (W-b: −44.76 pp, CI [−50.51, −36.42], p = 0.031), and none decelerated. In this action space a plan that stays put at rest is a plan without acceleration, so this outcome is expected if the controls do not change, and by the rule it is not by itself evidence that the model uses the history.

What about the balanced checkpoint? There is no reading: `Balanced-6400` was rarely at low speed, and only three of its eight routes had 20 steps in both conditions. Supplementary Section S4.10 also gives the full protocol, a sensitivity check and descriptive results for a second replacement, a stationary history on steps where the vehicle was moving, which carry no reading.

Limits. The test replaced the ego-history tokens only; the camera window, which also shows whether the vehicle moves, was unchanged, so we did not test a visual route to the same behaviour. A replaced step measures the immediate plan, not how long a stop lasts. The test has eight routes, one pass, unseeded sampling and no reference model: `Balanced-6400` gave no reading and we did not test the unadapted policy. Three readings of W-a are not separated: a policy that uses its speed correctly would also plan to accelerate less often when its history reports 4.0 m/s; the class of plans that hold speed covers staying at rest under the true history and keeping 4.0 m/s under the moving one, which are different behaviours; and a moving history with camera frames that show no motion is an input the model was not trained on, so the outcome may be its response to an input it has not seen.



5. Discussion and Limitations

The two checkpoints of the main pair differed by −26.83 pp over both passes in how often they returned a near-stationary plan, and a difference in stay-put plans in the same direction was present on identical replayed inputs. With one checkpoint per mix the effect of the mix cannot be separated from that of the training run; a second pass addresses evaluation noise only. Two observations bear on this, and neither removes it. `Decoupling-6400`, trained in a separate run, returned near-stationary plans about as rarely as the balanced checkpoint, but its set shares 86.3 % of its samples with the balanced set, so this is closer to a second run on nearly the same data than to an independent test. The unadapted policy lay between the two checkpoints, which gives the direction of each relative to its starting point and does not bear on variation between training runs. Of the three fine-tuned checkpoints only `Hazard-heavy-6400` had a rate of near-stationary plans detectably above that of the unadapted policy in closed loop (Section 4.6; Table 6). Each mix still has one seed, and the two remaining factors are bundled.

The behaviour matches the description of the inertia problem by Codevilla et al. [5]: a policy that, once slow, keeps predicting that it stays where it is. The match is behavioural. It is not shown that the stops of `Hazard-heavy-6400` were unwarranted, and on the replayed states `Balanced-6400` stayed put less often than the demonstrations themselves, so the comparison ranks the checkpoints by how often they stay put and not by how well they drive. The behaviour is consistent with the conjecture of Section 3.2, which does not account for `Balanced-6400` planning to stay put far less often than its own training share; coarse sampling is a rival mechanism that we did not test. Since a plan that stays put at rest is a plan without acceleration, the difference can also be read as one in how often the model issues a start.

In the exploratory history-swap test the learned controls of `Hazard-heavy-6400` depended on its ego history, by the rule fixed in advance. We report this outcome as found; it does not identify why the checkpoint stays put (Section 4.7 gives the limits).

The controller decides how much of the difference in plans becomes a difference in standstill. Without the gate the difference in full stops shrank from −23.54 pp to −4.65 pp, with an interval that includes zero while the rank test still rejects, and the differences in near-stationary plans and in slow driving remained (Section 4.4). This supports an origin of the difference in the plans, with the gate amplifying its expression as full stops (exploratory, one pass). Because the gate may also feed back on the plans, the closed-loop rate of near-stationary plans describes the model together with its controller. Removing the gate was not a remedy: the hazard-heavy checkpoint then completed more of each route with more collision events and no detectable change in Driving Score, the pattern that Que et al. [40] report for a release rule on a different planner. Neither a held stop nor a blocked vehicle solves those scenarios.

On the road, the balanced checkpoint had a higher Driving Efficiency (post hoc) at a Driving Score that did not differ significantly. Over both passes it completed more of each route, its Driving Score did not differ significantly (+3.96 points), and its Driving Efficiency was higher by 22.89 points (Section 4.3). The efficiency comparison is post hoc and descriptive: we added it after the Driving Score had shown no detectable difference, the measure leaves out a final stop and does not count blocked time as a cost, and the balanced checkpoint had more blocked time. Collision events did not differ detectably over both passes, per route or per distance driven. Faster is not the same as better. We therefore describe the balanced checkpoint as more efficient by this post hoc measure, at a Driving Score and a collision count that were not detectably different, and not as driving better. With three checkpoints, no trade-off curve is described. For this pair the results are consistent with a trade-off in which the checkpoint that stands still less advances farther and spends more time commanded to move without moving, in most cases within 10 m of a recorded collision position (Section 4.3). Against the hazard-heavy checkpoint, `Decoupling-6400` showed the same pattern of fewer near-stationary plans, more route completion and more blocked time, with no detectable difference in Driving Score and with more collision events per route (Section 4.5). The pattern is specific to these contrasts: `Balanced-6400` stood still less than the unadapted policy without completing detectably more of each route (Table 6; Supplementary Section S4.9). Whether a stop was required is not known from the logs, so less standstill cannot be read as better; we recommend neither mix over the other and make no safety claim. For evaluation practice, we recommend reporting standstill split by command and next to collisions. A several-fold difference in plans did not show in the Driving Score, and a comparison on that score needs repeated passes: a gain in the first pass did not recur in the second.

Limitations.

Design. Each condition is one checkpoint from one training run (seed 42). The intervals describe route-to-route variation for these checkpoints, not variation between training runs. All results concern fine-tuning sets of 6,400 samples; the dependence on the size of the fine-tuning set is outside the scope of this paper. The mixes differ in group mix, lateral balance and stationary share together; the decoupling checkpoint separates only the scenario-group mix and has one seed and one pass. The study covers one policy, one simulator and one controller.

Evaluation. Closed-loop runs are not deterministic: between the two passes of a checkpoint the per-route standstill differed with a standard deviation of 18 to 25 pp (Section 4.1). One server process served all routes of a job without clearing its streaming cache, and two processes of the same model did not return identical plans on all replayed states, for a reason we did not find, so a difference between two jobs includes a difference between two server processes. The second pass and the same-input replay address this for the main pair; the results with one pass (the decoupling checkpoint, the runs without the gate and the unadapted policy) remain exposed to it, and their contrasts in Driving Score and collision events are descriptive. The 44 routes are a subset of the benchmark, seven are touched by training samples, and the controller was tuned on 20 of them with a fine-tuned checkpoint, which can favour fine-tuned models over the unadapted one. The gate-off controller was written after first results of these checkpoints were known. The 60 s standstill rule censors the standstill fraction. With this subset, the custom controller, Low rendering quality and CARLA 0.9.15, the Driving Scores and the Driving Efficiency are not comparable with published 220-route results.

Measurement and transfer. Blocked time is inferred from the command log, not from a contact sensor, and the bridge logs neither signal state nor actor distances, so the logs do not show whether a stop was required. Scripted CARLA traffic does not react to a waiting or blocked ego vehicle as human drivers would, so the costs that either behaviour imposes on others are not represented. Inference uses a VLM quantized to 4-bit weights and 8-bit activations with streaming cache reuse, whereas training uses full-context BF16. The training data were collected at rendering quality Epic (taken from the collection code; no log of the collection run is available) and evaluation ran at Low, a visual domain shift that may affect all fine-tuned models and could moderate the differences reported here. We made no comparison with controller-side anti-stall rules or model-side remedies such as history dropout [9] or speed prediction [5].

Pre-specification. We fixed the tests after preliminary evaluations of the same checkpoints on the same routes (Section 4.1). They are pre-specified tests on new runs, not tests of hypotheses formed without sight of related data, and the route set was not held out.

Future work follows from these limits: further training seeds, a direct measurement of the stay-put sampling rate, the remaining routes, logging of contact and hazards, and comparisons with history dropout and a creep-style controller.


6. Conclusion

We compared two fine-tuning mixes, drawn from the same verified pool, for the flow-matching action expert of Alpamayo 1.5, with one training run per mix. The hazard-heavy checkpoint returned a near-stationary plan in 26.80 % of steps and the balanced one in 1.31 %. A test fixed before the runs was met (−25.49 pp, CI [−31.69, −19.63]), a repeat test on a second pass was met (−28.16 pp), and a difference in stay-put plans in the same direction was present on identical replayed inputs, where the unadapted policy lay between the two checkpoints, as it did in closed loop (Sections 4.2, 4.3 and 4.6). This behaviour matches the description of the inertia problem; we do not show that the stops were unwarranted. A third checkpoint, with the scenario-group shares of the hazard-heavy set and the manoeuvre mix and stationary share of the balanced set, returned near-stationary plans about as rarely as the balanced checkpoint (1.58 % of steps; we ran three of the four cells of this design), so in these three training runs the rate went with the manoeuvre mix and the stationary share. On the road the balanced checkpoint stood still about half as long and completed more of each route. Its Driving Efficiency, the speed of the ego vehicle relative to the surrounding traffic, was higher (+22.89 points; post hoc), at a Driving Score that did not differ significantly (+3.96 points over both passes); a gain in Driving Score seen in the first pass did not recur in the second. The efficiency measure leaves out a final stop and does not count blocked time, and the balanced checkpoint spent more time commanded to accelerate without moving. Collision events did not differ detectably over both passes, per route (+0.24) or per distance driven (Section 4.3). Faster is not the same as better. Without the controller's standstill gate the difference in full stops shrank to −4.65 pp, with an interval that includes zero while the rank test still rejects, and the difference in near-stationary plans remained (−13.99 pp; Section 4.4), which supports an origin of the difference in the plans, with the gate turning short plans into held stops (exploratory, one pass). By the rule fixed in advance the checkpoint trained on the decoupling mix had fewer near-stationary plans at a cost (one training run), and the mix is not called a remedy. In an exploratory test on six routes the learned controls of the hazard-heavy checkpoint depended on its ego-motion history (plans that hold speed: +32.00 pp under a history that reports motion; Section 4.7). The test does not identify the mechanism: the pre-specified condition for tying the stay-put plans to a history that reports rest was not met, correct use of speed would give the same outcome, and the camera input was not varied. For evaluation practice, we recommend reporting standstill time split by command and repeating passes for any comparison on Driving Score. With one checkpoint per mix the dependence on composition across training seeds is untested. We make no safety claim.
