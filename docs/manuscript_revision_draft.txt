The Inertia Problem in a Fine-Tuned Vision-Language-Action Driving Policy: A Closed-Loop Comparison of Checkpoints Trained on Different Data Mixes

Abstract

Imitation-learned driving policies can learn that a vehicle at rest stays at rest, the inertia problem. We ask whether checkpoints of a vision-language-action policy fine-tuned on different data mixes differ in this behaviour. With one training run per mix, we fine-tuned the flow-matching action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 on a balanced mix and on a hazard-heavy mix with more stationary samples, drawn from one pool of CARLA expert data, and evaluated the checkpoints in closed loop on 44 Bench2Drive routes. At 6,400 samples the hazard-heavy checkpoint returned a near-stationary plan (planned path below 1.5 m, the threshold of the controller's standstill gate) in 26.80 % of steps and the balanced checkpoint in 1.31 % (pre-specified test: −25.49 percentage points, 95 % CI [−31.69, −19.63]); the unadapted policy lay between them at 13.24 %. The difference recurred in a second pass and on identical replayed inputs. The balanced checkpoint stood still about half as long and completed more of each route, but spent more time commanded to accelerate without moving; its Driving Score and collision events per route were not detectably different. Post hoc, its Bench2Drive Driving Efficiency was higher (+22.89 points, CI [6.14, 34.89]). In an exploratory run without the standstill gate the difference in full stops shrank from −23.54 to −4.65 percentage points, while the difference in plans remained. A third checkpoint, which separates the scenario-group shares from the manoeuvre mix and stationary share, returned such plans about as rarely as the balanced one. At 1,600 samples the differences were small and at the border of detection. In an exploratory test the planned accelerations of the hazard-heavy checkpoint depended on its ego-motion history; the test identifies no mechanism. Variation between training runs is untested, no stop is shown to be unwarranted, and we make no safety claim.

**Keywords:** vision-language-action models; flow matching; imitation learning; fine-tuning data composition; low-speed planning; closed-loop evaluation; autonomous driving


1. Introduction

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for driving combine a pretrained vision-language model (VLM), which encodes camera images, ego-motion history and a navigation instruction, with an action head that outputs a future trajectory. In designs such as $\pi_0$ [1], $\pi_{0.5}$ [2] and NVIDIA's Alpamayo family [3, 4], the action head is a separate flow-matching [5] expert that turns a noise sample into a trajectory in a few solver steps. Unlike a regression head, it can represent several distinct futures for one scene and returns one of them at each call.

Such a policy is commonly adapted to a new simulator or vehicle by supervised fine-tuning of the action expert with the VLM frozen. A natural instinct is to add to the fine-tuning set more of what is hard: obstacles, hazards, emergency stops. In the hazard-heavy mix we study, the share of stationary samples was also set higher, and among its low-speed samples the expert stays put in 31 %, against 11 % in the balanced mix. Whether this share changes what the fine-tuned policy plans is the question of this study. Standing still when the road is clear and making contact with an obstacle are two different failures. We measure standstill and contact, cannot tell from our logs whether a given stop was required, assume no relation between the two, and cannot place either checkpoint relative to safety.

Imitation-learned driving policies that observe their own motion can learn that a vehicle at rest tends to stay at rest. Codevilla et al. [6] named this the inertia problem; it is a form of causal confusion [7] and of the copycat behaviour described for policies trained on observation histories [8, 9]. The remedies proposed so far act on the model input, the loss or the weighting of training frames [6, 9, 10]. Robot policies have been reported to freeze or idle because of what their training data contain [11, 12], and Alpamayo 1.5 has been reported to get stuck in CARLA [13].

We have not found a study that changes only the composition of the fine-tuning set of such a policy, holds the model, the loss, the training schedule and the controller fixed, and measures the result in closed loop at the level of the plans the model produces. Without the last step the question cannot be answered, because standstill in closed loop is a joint product of the model and of the controller that executes its plans.

We fine-tuned the 2.28B-parameter action expert of Alpamayo 1.5 on two mixes drawn from one verified pool of CARLA expert samples. The balanced mix targets 50 % straight driving, symmetric lateral manoeuvres and 2 % stationary samples; the hazard-heavy mix targets a larger share of obstacle and hazard scenario groups, a left-skewed lateral distribution and 10 % stationary samples. Each is built at 1,600 and 6,400 samples, with identical training schedules at each size, and the models are evaluated in closed loop on 44 Bench2Drive routes [14]. We then repeated the evaluation of both 6,400-sample models, ran both with the controller's standstill gate removed, analysed the logged plan of every step, trained a third checkpoint that separates one of the three factors in which the mixes differ, and tested whether the plans depend on the ego-motion history.

The study has four groups of tests, labelled F1, F2, R1 to R3 and N1 to N3 in Section 4; Section 4.1 states when each was fixed.
- **Plans (primary):** at 6,400 samples and a matched training schedule, the balanced-mix checkpoint (`Balanced-6400`) returns a near-stationary plan, with a predicted path shorter than 1.5 m, in a smaller share of steps than the hazard-heavy-mix checkpoint (`Hazard-heavy-6400`).
- **Standstill time:** `Balanced-6400` spends less route time below 0.1 m/s than `Hazard-heavy-6400`.
- **Repeat test:** in a second pass of the same two checkpoints the difference in near-stationary plans recurs, as do the differences in blocked time (time at rest while the controller commands acceleration) and in the standstill outside it.
- **Dataset size:** the difference in near-stationary plans is also present at 1,600 samples.

The first three were fixed before any of their runs. The fourth was fixed while its second run was in progress, before any value of either run was computed. The readings of four further analyses were also fixed before their data: the same-input replay, the decoupling checkpoint, the run without the gate and the history-swap test. Each of the four had been run once in the preliminary evaluations, with its outcome known, and its rule was not changed. All other analyses are labelled post hoc or exploratory.

We found four things.
- **The two checkpoints differed strongly in how often they returned a near-stationary plan.** `Hazard-heavy-6400` returned a near-stationary plan in 26.80 % of steps and `Balanced-6400` in 1.31 % (pre-specified test: −25.49 percentage points, 95 % CI [−31.69, −19.63]), and the difference recurred in a second pass (−28.16 pp; Section 4.3). On identical replayed inputs the stay-put share was 17.61 % against 0.35 %, with the unadapted policy between the two at 3.28 % (Section 4.2), so the difference belongs to the experts and not only to the states each of them reached. In closed loop the unadapted policy returned a near-stationary plan in 13.24 % of steps (Section 4.7). The 1.5 m threshold is that of the controller's standstill gate, so the closed-loop rate is taken with the gate active; without the gate the same two checkpoints returned such a plan in 14.58 % and 0.60 % of steps (Section 4.4). A third checkpoint, trained on the scenario-group shares of the hazard-heavy set with the manoeuvre mix and stationary share of the balanced set, returned near-stationary plans about as rarely as the balanced one (1.58 % of steps against 1.22 %; Section 4.5). At 1,600 samples the difference was far smaller and at the border of detection (8.34 % against 4.92 %; −3.42 pp; Section 4.6).
- **The behaviour matches the description of the inertia problem [6]; we do not show that the stops were unwarranted.** The checkpoint trained on more stationary samples stayed put more often once slow, which is the behaviour that Codevilla et al. describe. Our logs do not show whether a given stop was required, and on the replayed states the balanced checkpoint stayed put less often than the expert demonstrations did (0.63 % of plans against 3.91 % of demonstrated futures, both pooled over the 384 states; Section 4.2), so staying put less is not shown to be better.
- **In an exploratory test, the planned accelerations of the hazard-heavy checkpoint depended on its ego-motion history.** The test, on six routes, replaced the ego history of `Hazard-heavy-6400` at low speed by one that reports motion; the share of plans that hold their speed rose by 32.00 pp (Section 4.8). By the rule fixed in advance, the learned controls of this checkpoint depend on its ego history. The test does not identify the mechanism: the rule's condition for tying the stay-put plans to a history that reports rest was not met, a policy that uses its speed correctly could give the same outcome, and the camera input was not varied.
- **Less standstill came with more progress and more blocked time, not with a higher Driving Score; collision events did not differ detectably.** Over both passes `Balanced-6400` stood still about half as long (−23.54 pp) and completed more of each route (+6.84 pp). It also spent more time commanded to accelerate without moving (+14.29 pp), and its Driving Score was not detectably higher (+3.96 points, CI [−3.06, 11.67]). Its collision events per route did not differ detectably (0.73 against 0.49; +0.24, CI [0.00, 0.43]; 28 against 28 events in the first pass and 36 against 15 in the second), nor did its collision events per distance driven (ratio 1.29; Section 4.3). Without the controller's standstill gate the difference in full stops shrank to −4.65 pp (CI [−11.44, 3.20]), while the difference in near-stationary plans (−13.99 pp) and in slow driving remained (Section 4.4), which supports an origin of the difference in the plans, with the gate turning short plans into held stops (exploratory, one pass). We make no safety claim in either direction.

The paper contributes (i) a controlled comparison of the low-speed behaviour of checkpoints of one flow-matching VLA driving policy that were fine-tuned once each on different data compositions, with the model, loss, schedule and controller held fixed; (ii) measures at the level of the plan, the motion and the command (stay-put plans by speed, crawling, blocked time) that separate what the model plans from what the controller makes of it, with a same-input replay and a closed-loop run without the gate to check them; (iii) the observation that, in this pair, the checkpoint that stood still less made more progress, spent more time blocked and did not obtain a detectably higher Driving Score; (iv) a loading check for serving fine-tuned action experts with the FlashDrive inference engine [15]: the engine fuses projection weights of the expert, so a checkpoint that is loaded by tensor name after this fusion, as a simple serving script does, leaves part of the fine-tuned weights unloaded without an error (Section 3.4). The five datasets are released (Section 6).

The limits are those of the design: one training seed per condition, two mixes that differ in three factors at once (the third checkpoint separates one of them), one policy, one simulator, 44 routes and a controller tuned on 20 of them. With one training run per mix, the dependence on composition across training runs is untested, and a second evaluation pass addresses evaluation noise, not variation between training runs. At 1,600 samples the differences in plans and in standstill time were small and at the border of detection (Section 4.6).

2. Related Work

End-to-end driving and language. End-to-end planners [16, 17, 18, 19, 20] output a trajectory by regression or by scoring candidates. DriveLM [21], LMDrive [22] and SimLingo [23] connect language with driving. Alpamayo-R1 [3] and Alpamayo 1.5 [4] combine chain-of-causation reasoning with a flow-matching action expert; we use the latter.

Generative action heads. Diffusion Policy [24] and DiffusionDrive [25] model action sequences by denoising diffusion, and $\pi_0$ [1] attaches a flow-matching action expert to a pretrained VLM. Flow matching [5] regresses a velocity field along a probability path; with independent noise–data coupling the learned paths are not straight in general, and straighter paths need additional procedures [26, 27]. Such heads can represent several modes; we ask which mode they sample at low speed.

Inertia, copycat behaviour and their mitigations. Codevilla et al. [6] documented the inertia problem in behaviour cloning for driving, de Haan et al. [7] framed the broader failure as causal confusion, and Wen et al. [8] analysed copycat agents that exploit observation histories. Proposed remedies add a speed-prediction branch [6], upweight demonstration frames at expert action changes [9], apply dropout to the past-motion input [10] or predict a residual action [28]. Jaeger et al. [29] note that with waypoint outputs the steering signal is undefined when the vehicle is stopped, which requires additional heuristics in the controller. These remedies act on the model input, the loss, the weighting of training frames or the controller. Our intervention acts only on the composition of the fine-tuning set, and we did not compare it with these remedies (Section 5).

Idling, freezing and over-caution. All-zero actions in a robot dataset led a vision-language-action policy to freeze during evaluation [11], and policy idling in manipulation has been described as often a reflection of the training data [12]. In motion planning, the freezing robot problem [30, 31] denotes a planner that judges every forward path through a dense crowd unsafe and stops, and planners that only stay out of the way of predicted human trajectories tend toward defensive behaviour [32]. In CARLA, Jaeger et al. [29] identify a bias in which multimodal waypoint predictions are averaged longitudinally and the vehicle slows down. These works locate the tendency to stop in the data, in the output representation or in the planner.

Composition of imitation data. For robot policies, the quality [33], the mixture weights [34] and the diversity [35] of imitation datasets have been related to the learned policy. In driving, training on generated safety-critical scenarios improved collision avoidance [36], reweighting of training samples raised benchmark scores [37, 38], the style of the expert affected the trained policy [39], and Alpamayo 1.5 was reported to get stuck on a CARLA route [13]. These studies ask whether a change of data improves a benchmark score. We have not found a controlled change of the fine-tuning mix for such a model with low-speed behaviour in closed loop as the endpoint and the controller's part separated. Que et al. [40] report, for a camera-only planner on Bench2Drive with one seed, that 26 of 42 blocked routes ended with a learned brake output holding the vehicle while the planned trajectory asked for motion; a rule that released this hold reduced the blocked routes from 41 to 24 on the routes common to both runs and raised vehicle collisions from 0.44 to 0.62 per route, without a clear change in Driving Score. That study varies a controller-side rule; ours varies the fine-tuning data and removes the hold as an ablation.

Closed-loop evaluation. Errors of an imitation policy compound in closed loop [41], and open-loop planning metrics do not show this: on nuScenes [42] they can be matched by models that use only ego status [43, 44]. Repeated executions of the same scenario in CARLA [45] are not identical [46]. Bench2Drive [14] splits closed-loop evaluation into 220 short routes covering 44 scenario types.


3. Methodology


## 3.1 Problem Formulation and Dual-System Architecture

At control step $t$ the policy receives $\mathbf{x}_t = (\mathcal{I}_t, \mathcal{H}_t, c_t)$:
1. **Cameras:** $\mathcal{I}_t$ from four cameras (cross-left, front-wide and cross-right at $120^\circ$ field of view; front-telephoto at $30^\circ$) over a 4-frame window [4].
2. **Ego-motion history:** $\mathcal{H}_t = \{(\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{R}_i)\}_{i=0}^{15}$, 16 poses at $\Delta t = 0.1\text{ s}$ spacing over the last $1.5\text{ s}$ (the last pose is the current one), in the current ego frame ($x$ forward, $y$ left), tokenized into 48 history tokens (three per pose) that form part of the VLM input [3, 4].
3. **Navigation instruction:** $c_t$, a short text such as `Continue straight` or `Turn left in 11m` [4].

The policy outputs $K = 64$ future waypoints at the same $\Delta t = 0.1\text{ s}$ spacing ($6.4\text{ s}$ horizon; the first waypoint is $0.1\text{ s}$ ahead of the current pose), $\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_K]^\top$, $\mathbf{w}_k = (x_k, y_k, z_k)$, in the ego frame at time $t$ [3, 4]; the vertical coordinate $z_k$ is not modelled.

Alpamayo 1.5 (about 10B parameters) consists of an 8B-parameter VLM backbone $\Phi_{\text{VLM}}$ (`nvidia/Cosmos-Reason2-8B`, Qwen3-VL architecture), which encodes $\mathbf{x}_t$ (the ego history included, as tokens) into a cached key-value state $\mathbf{e}_t$, and a 2.28B-parameter action expert $v_\theta$ that works in a unicycle acceleration–curvature action space [3, 4]. A trajectory is represented by normalized controls $\mathbf{A}_1 = [\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_K]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 2}$, $\mathbf{u}_k = \big((a_k - \mu_a)/\sigma_a,\, (\kappa_k - \mu_\kappa)/\sigma_\kappa\big)$ for acceleration $a_k$ and curvature $\kappa_k$, with $(\mu_a, \sigma_a) = (0.0290, 0.6810)\text{ m/s}^2$ and $(\mu_\kappa, \sigma_\kappa) = (0.000269, 0.0261)\text{ m}^{-1}$. Controls are mapped to waypoints by unicycle integration with trapezoidal quadrature [3, 4], starting at the ego origin with zero heading and at an initial speed $v_0$ that the model estimates from $\mathcal{H}_t$ (it is not a learned output):
$$v_k = v_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k} a_j \Delta t, \quad \psi_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \kappa_j \left(v_{j-1}\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}a_j \Delta t^2\right)$$
$$x_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\big(v_{j-1}\cos\psi_{j-1} + v_j\cos\psi_j\big), \quad y_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\big(v_{j-1}\sin\psi_{j-1} + v_j\sin\psi_j\big)$$
We call $L = \sum_{k=2}^{K} \lVert (x_k, y_k) - (x_{k-1}, y_{k-1}) \rVert_2$ the predicted trajectory extent: the planar path length from the first to the last waypoint (63 segments; the segment from the ego position to the first waypoint is not included). Because the integration starts at $v_0$, $L$ depends on the current speed as well as on the learned controls: with every control at its training mean, a plan from rest has $L = 0.59\text{ m}$, and a plan from $v_0 = 0.4\text{ m/s}$ has $L = 3.1\text{ m}$. Statements about $L$ at low speed therefore condition on speed (Section 4.3). Supplementary Section S3.1 gives a numerical check of these equations.

The action expert is trained by conditional flow matching with a linear interpolation path and independent coupling [5]: $\mathbf{A}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ is drawn independently of the demonstration $\mathbf{A}_1$, $\mathbf{A}_s = (1-s)\mathbf{A}_0 + s\mathbf{A}_1$ for $s \in [0,1]$, and the network regresses the conditional velocity $\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0$:
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim p(s),\, \mathbf{A}_0,\, (\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{A}_1) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \lVert v_\theta(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t) - (\mathbf{A}_1 - \mathbf{A}_0) \rVert_2^2 \right]$$
Here $p(s)$ is the flow-time distribution of the training recipe [47], which we did not change ([3] describes a shifted beta distribution; we did not verify the distribution in the recipe version we ran). At inference the expert integrates $d\mathbf{A}_s/ds = v_\theta(\mathbf{A}_s, s \mid \mathbf{e}_t)$ from a noise sample at $s = 0$ to $s = 1$. The expert sees the ego history only through $\mathbf{e}_t$; the history acts a second time through $v_0$ in the integration above.

## 3.2 Why Data Composition Could Change Low-Speed Plans: A Sampling Conjecture

The minimiser of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}$ is the marginal velocity field, and integrating it from Gaussian noise transports the noise distribution to the training conditional $p_{\text{train}}(\mathbf{A}_1 \mid \mathbf{e}_t)$ in the limit of exact fit and exact integration [5]. In that limit each inference call returns a sample from the conditional distribution, not its mean, so a well-fitted expert returns each kind of future about as often as the training data contain it in a given context. The deployed sampler is far from the limit: it evaluates the expert four times per call, and a coarse integration can pull samples toward the conditional mean, a rival mechanism for short plans that we did not test.

The relevant contexts are low-speed ones, where the camera image and the ego history are often compatible both with staying put and with starting to move. For samples with ego speed $\le 0.5\text{ m/s}$ we define three classes of future by the path length $L$ of the $6.4\text{ s}$ trajectory: stays put ($L < 0.64\text{ m}$, a planned mean speed below $0.1\text{ m/s}$), starts moving ($L \ge 2\text{ m}$), and in between.

**Conjecture (stay-put sampling).** A flow-matching expert trained on data in which a larger share of low-speed samples stay put will, in low-speed closed-loop contexts, sample stay-put trajectories more often. Because the vehicle then remains at low speed, the next query again sees a low-speed context, so repeated stay-put samples extend the time at rest after the reason for stopping has gone.

The conjecture is not one of the tests of this study. It was formulated after first closed-loop results of these checkpoints were known, so their agreement with it is not a test of it. The relevant share is the conditional one among low-speed samples (Table 1): 30.9 % of them stay put in the hazard-heavy 6,400-sample set and 10.9 % in the balanced one, and nearly the same shares at 1,600 samples. The conjecture does not predict the 1,600-sample result, where the shares differ almost as much and the checkpoints differ far less (Section 4.6), so it is at most part of the explanation. Supplementary Section S3.2 gives the argument in full, with a further condition that we did not measure and the rival explanations that the design cannot separate.

## 3.3 Fine-Tuning Datasets

Demonstrations come from the LEAD [48] privileged expert, recorded in CARLA 0.9.16 at rendering quality Epic. The four datasets of the main design, two mixes at two sizes, are drawn from one verified training pool of 34,132 samples; we call them the Matrix datasets. Each sample is labelled with a scenario group (A–F) and a navigation manoeuvre. A sample is stationary if the ego vehicle moves less than 0.5 m over its 6.4 s future.

Each mix, or regime, is specified by target marginals: the group shares, the manoeuvre shares and the stationary share (Table 1). Each 1,600-sample set is the exact per-bucket first quarter of the 6,400-sample set of the same regime. The balanced and hazard-heavy 6,400-sample sets share 4,284 samples (66.9 % of each). All datasets share one validation split of 1,567 samples.

The two regimes differ in three factors at once (group mix, lateral balance and stationary share), and in this pool the factors are coupled, so comparisons between regimes estimate the effect of the bundle. A fifth dataset, `floodedgroups_balanced_6400`, has the scenario-group shares of the hazard-heavy mix with the manoeuvre shares and the 2 % stationary share of the balanced mix. We call it the decoupling mix; it separates the group mix from the lateral-plus-stationary bundle. One model, `Decoupling-6400`, was trained on it with the recipe and schedule of the other 6,400-sample models (Section 4.5).

Training and evaluation routes overlap: training samples in the Matrix sets touch seven of the 44 evaluation routes we use (Table 1 gives the number of such samples per set). Because the overlap is of similar size in both regimes, it bears mainly on comparisons against the unadapted Alpamayo 1.5, which we call `Base`. Section 4.3 reports the two main contrasts on the 37 untouched routes (values in Supplementary Section S4.4), and Supplementary Section S3.3 gives the scenario groups, the construction and the overlap in full.

## 3.4 Fine-Tuning, Inference and Controller

Parameter isolation. The VLM backbone is frozen during fine-tuning [3, 47]; only the action expert is trained, with the Alpamayo Stage-2 recipe [47]. For deployment on one 24 GB GPU the frozen VLM language layers are quantized to 4-bit weights with pairwise rotation quantization (ParoQuant) [49] and run with 8-bit activations in our model server, which is built on the FlashDrive inference engine [15]; the action expert runs in `bfloat16`.

Schedules. All four Matrix models and the decoupling model used an effective batch of 8, 10 epochs and seed 42, which gives 2,000 optimizer steps at 1,600 samples and 8,000 at 6,400 samples. The two 6,400-sample Matrix models have identical configurations except for the dataset name and trained without interruption; the two 1,600-sample models share a schedule but were each interrupted and resumed (Supplementary Table S1).

Inference. The model server, built on the FlashDrive engine [15], keeps a sliding-window key-value cache and integrates the learned field in eight uniform forward-Euler steps. The server code we ran reuses the velocity on four of the eight steps, so the expert is evaluated four times per call; we did not measure the discretisation error. The server does not seed its noise draws, so closed-loop runs are not reproducible step for step (Section 4.1). CARLA runs synchronously at 20 Hz, and at every step the agent queries the server and acts on the plan it returns.

Loading of the fine-tuned expert. The FlashDrive release contains the inference engine and no server for fine-tuned experts; the serving script is ours. A serving script that first loads the base model and then copies the tensors of a fine-tuned action expert into it by name, skipping names that find no match, does not load the whole expert: because the engine merges projections of each expert layer, 180 of the 396 expert-layer tensors would keep their base values, without a message (Supplementary Section S3.4). We therefore serve every fine-tuned checkpoint with a full-loading server. It refuses to start unless all 416 tensors of the checkpoint file are found in memory with a largest difference of 0, and stores a report of this check with every route. `Base` needs no such step.

Controller. All runs except those of Sections 4.4 and 4.8 use the same bridge controller (Figure 1). CARLA's `VehiclePIDController` tracks the predicted trajectory towards a lookahead waypoint, with a target speed that follows the planned speed up to 35 km/h. The target speed, the gate and every extent-based measure in this paper are read from the predicted waypoints, whose speed information Jaeger et al. [29] found to be ambiguous for multimodal plans. A standstill gate sets the target speed and the throttle to zero and applies the brake whenever
$$(L < 1.5\text{ m}) \;\lor\; (v < 0.5\text{ m/s} \land L < 2.0\text{ m}).$$
The gate keeps no internal state, and its second clause makes it harder to leave rest. When the gate does not fire, the speed is below 1.5 m/s, the target speed exceeds 4 km/h and $L \ge 2.5\text{ m}$, the throttle is raised to at least 0.35 and the brake released (a starting torque). Supplementary Section S3.4 gives the controller in full.

The PID gains and lookahead were tuned before this comparison on 20 of the 44 evaluation routes, with a fine-tuned checkpoint that is not part of this study; Section 4.3 reports the two main contrasts on the other 24 routes (values in Supplementary Section S4.4). Standstill is a property of controller and model together, and Section 4.4 separates their contributions with a run without the gate.

4. Experimental Results


## 4.1 Evaluation Protocol and Disclosures

Routes and simulator. We use 44 Bench2Drive routes, one per scenario type; at about 22 minutes per route, the full 220-route suite was not run for every model. Because of this subset and the custom controller, the Driving Scores reported here are not comparable with published 220-route Bench2Drive results. Evaluation ran in CARLA 0.9.15, in synchronous mode at 20 Hz and at rendering quality Low.

Runs and stages. Every fine-tuned checkpoint was served by the full-loading server of Section 3.4. All runs after the first pass of the two 6,400-sample checkpoints formed a second stage, which was to follow if the plan-level test of the first pass was met, as it was. One server process served all routes of a job, and its streaming cache was not cleared between routes; this is the same for every checkpoint and pass.

Termination rule and censoring. The harness ends a route after 4,000 ticks (200 s) or after 1,200 consecutive ticks (60 s) with ego speed below 0.1 m/s. The standstill fraction is therefore censored: a model that stops for good accrues at most 60 s of final standstill. The unadapted policy was evaluated under the same rule. We call this run `Base-Rerun`, because a preliminary run of `Base` predates the rule, and every closed-loop comparison with the unadapted policy uses it.

Non-determinism. The model server does not seed its noise draws, so two runs of the same checkpoint differ from the first step. Between the two passes of each 6,400-sample checkpoint the per-route standstill difference had a standard deviation of 24.73 pp (`Balanced-6400`) and 18.29 pp (`Hazard-heavy-6400`) (post hoc). Per-route comparisons and small strata are therefore descriptive only.

Collisions. The leaderboard merges repeated contacts with the same actor before logging; we additionally merge, within each route, entries with the same actor identifier and report these as collision events.

Pre-specification. (i) The tests of the first pass (F1, F2) and the repeat rule for the second pass (R1 to R3), all defined in Section 4.3, were committed before the first route of the evaluation was run. The checkpoints, the routes and the measures had been used in preliminary evaluations before that, so these are pre-specified tests on new runs, not a registration prior to all data. (ii) The readings of four further analyses were also fixed before their data: the same-input replay, the decoupling checkpoint, the run without the gate and the history-swap test. Each of these four analyses had been run once in the preliminary evaluations, and its outcome was known before the runs reported in this paper; the rules were not changed. (iii) The tests of the 1,600-sample pair (N1 to N3, defined in Section 4.6) were committed while its second run was in progress, before any value of either run was computed. (iv) All other analyses are post hoc or exploratory and are labelled as such. No decision rule was changed after any look at incomplete data. The rules are sections of one file in the project repository, cited below as ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM with a section number, with commit times that were not time-stamped by a third party; Supplementary Section S4.1 gives the full record with its dates.

Statistics. Route-level differences are paired over the 44 routes, or over the number of routes given with a result. Tests are two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and intervals are two-sided 95 % BCa bootstrap intervals for the mean paired difference. A rank test and an interval for the mean can disagree, and we say so where they do. For blocked time, which is zero on most routes, we also give an exact sign-flip permutation test of the mean difference. Differences in percentages are given in percentage points (pp). Holm adjustment is made over the two tests named with each rule; all other p-values are unadjusted. Each condition is one trained checkpoint (seed 42), so every test compares two specific checkpoints across routes and says nothing about variation between training runs. Table 2 defines the measures, and Supplementary Section S4.1 gives further detail.

## 4.2 Same-Input Replay

Closed-loop rates of plans are taken in the states each model reached, so they do not compare the experts on the same inputs. We therefore replayed recorded states through the engine of the model server. The states are the 384 samples of the shared validation split with a speed of 0.5 m/s or less, from 31 source logs; the split is held out by route from every training set. For each state the engine returned 16 sampled plans, with a random seed fixed per state and the same for every expert. The outcome is the stay-put share, the share of the 16 plans with $L < 0.64$ m, averaged within each source log and compared over the 31 logs. The comparison was committed before any inference of the replay (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §21). The statistic reported here and its reading were fixed before any of the runs of this section (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §24), with the outcome of a preliminary replay known. In the demonstrations the future stays put in 15 of the 384 states (3.91 %).

Do the experts differ on identical inputs? They do. `Balanced-6400` had a stay-put share of 0.35 % and `Hazard-heavy-6400` of 17.61 %, as means over the 31 source logs (**−17.26 pp**, CI [−23.78, −12.13], p = $5.6 \times 10^{-6}$). Pooled over the 384 states the shares are 0.63 % and 22.71 %. The rule fixed in advance reads a negative difference with an interval below zero as a difference between the two experts on identical inputs, for low-speed states reached by the demonstrator, and an interval that includes zero as no shown difference. By this rule the two experts differ on identical inputs.

In which direction did fine-tuning move the policy? In both, in these two checkpoints (descriptive). `Base` returned stay-put plans in 3.28 %. Against it, `Balanced-6400` was lower (−2.92 pp, CI [−4.60, −1.95]) and `Hazard-heavy-6400` higher (+14.33 pp, CI [9.46, 20.62]). On these states, the one training run on the balanced mix therefore lowered the stay-put share of the base model and the one on the hazard-heavy mix raised it; with one run per mix this shows the direction of each checkpoint and not an effect of the mix. Supplementary Section S4.3 gives the decoupling checkpoint and the 1,600-sample pair.

What do the plans look like on a single state? Figure 2 shows the 16 plans of the three 6,400-sample checkpoints on two replayed states, chosen by a rule fixed before any state was looked at (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §27). It is an illustration without a test. Panel (b) is the extreme of the set, not a typical state, and for neither state do we know which plan is right.

Is the replay reproducible? Within a process it is; between processes, not fully. A later process of the unchanged base model had a stay-put share of 4.23 % against 3.28 % for the first, and we do not know the cause. The comparisons of this section therefore carry an uncertainty of about 1 pp, estimated from one pair of processes of one model. It is small against the difference between the two 6,400-sample checkpoints and not small against the smaller contrasts of Supplementary Section S4.3, which we report without relying on them. The contrast between `Balanced-6400` and `Base` holds against the later base process as well (−3.88 pp, CI [−5.74, −2.59]). Supplementary Section S4.3 also gives the full protocol and all checks.

What does the replay not show? The states are those a privileged expert driver reached; states that only the learned policies reach, such as those after a collision, are absent. Each state was replayed as two consecutive frame windows, as at the start of streaming deployment (Supplementary Section S4.3), not as a long stream. The replay shows that the experts differ on the same inputs. It does not show why, and with one training run per mix it cannot attribute the difference to the data mix.


## 4.3 The Matched Pair at 6,400 Samples

Protocol and tests fixed before the runs. The two 6,400-sample checkpoints were evaluated on the 44 routes in a first and a second pass, and all routes were scored and counted (Supplementary Section S4.4). F1, the primary test, is the share of logged steps with a near-stationary plan ($L < 1.5$ m), `Balanced-6400` minus `Hazard-heavy-6400`, paired by route. It is met if the difference is negative, the interval lies below zero and p < 0.05. F2 is the standstill time, read by cases fixed in advance: an interval below zero with a point estimate of −4.0 pp or lower counts as a strong result. Both are Holm-adjusted over the two. By the repeat rule, decided on the second pass alone, the share of near-stationary plans (R1), the standstill outside blocked time (R2) and the blocked time (R3) each count as replicated if the difference has the sign of the first pass, its interval excludes zero and p < 0.05, with Holm adjustment over R2 and R3. All other comparisons in this section are descriptive.

Does the balanced checkpoint return near-stationary plans less often? It does, by a wide margin (Table 3; Figure 3 shows both passes pooled). `Balanced-6400` returned a near-stationary plan in 1.31 % of steps and `Hazard-heavy-6400` in 26.80 %, a difference of **−25.49 pp** (CI [−31.69, −19.63], p = $1.1 \times 10^{-13}$, Holm $2.3 \times 10^{-13}$; **F1 is met**). Among the steps at 0.5 m/s or less the difference in stay-put plans was of the same size (Table 3), in the direction of the shares on identical inputs (Section 4.2).

Does the vehicle stand still less? It does. `Balanced-6400` stood still for 22.97 % of the route time and `Hazard-heavy-6400` for 46.66 %, a difference of **−23.69 pp** (CI [−33.48, −15.08], p = $3.3 \times 10^{-6}$, Holm $3.3 \times 10^{-6}$; **F2 is a strong result** by the rule fixed in advance). `Hazard-heavy-6400` also spent more time crawling and drove at less than half the mean speed (Table 3).

Is the standstill of the two checkpoints of the same kind? It is not. `Hazard-heavy-6400` had no blocked time on any route: none of its standstill was time in which the controller commanded acceleration for 5 s or more and the vehicle did not move. `Balanced-6400` had blocked time on 10 routes (12.69 % of steps on average; sign-flip permutation test p = 0.002). Outside blocked time it stood still for 10.28 % of steps against 46.66 % (−36.38 pp, CI [−45.55, −28.16]). Part of the standstill that the balanced checkpoint kept was thus time in which it was commanded to move and did not.

What is the vehicle doing while it is blocked? It keeps commanding forward motion, in most cases within 10 m of a recorded collision position (post hoc, descriptive). The plans at the blocked steps of `Balanced-6400` were forward plans: 2.0 m or longer in 97.7 % of them in the first pass and 99.7 % in the second. Of these steps, 91.4 % and 100.0 % lay within 10 m of a recorded collision position, a match that is weak evidence taken alone and not specific to blocked time. The records give neither the order of contact and stop nor the duration of contact. Blocked time is therefore co-located with recorded collisions, and whether it adds harm beyond the recorded contact cannot be measured from these logs (Supplementary Sections S4.2 and S4.5; Table S2). Blocked time is not a second form of staying put: the model asks for motion, so a controller rule that releases a held stop would not remove it.

Does the balanced checkpoint score higher on the benchmark? Not detectably. In the first pass `Balanced-6400` had the higher Driving Score (+10.52 points, CI [2.59, 19.09], p = 0.044). The outcome was not pre-specified, and it did not recur: the difference was −2.59 in the second pass (CI [−11.94, 6.51]) and +3.96 over both passes (CI [−3.06, 11.67], p = 0.36; Table 4). Route completion was higher for `Balanced-6400` over both passes (+6.84 pp, CI [0.80, 13.29], p = 0.0055). `Balanced-6400` had 28 and 36 collision events and `Hazard-heavy-6400` 28 and 15, a difference of +0.24 events per route over both passes (CI [0.00, 0.43], p = 0.024). Here the rank test rejects and the interval of the mean does not exclude zero. By a wording rule fixed before the runs of this paper (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §12a), one checkpoint is said to have more collisions only if the interval of the two-pass difference excludes zero; otherwise the counts are reported as not detectably different, as here. Per kilometre driven the ratio of events was 1.29, with an interval that includes one (CI [0.90, 1.92]). One pass of 44 routes was thus not enough to compare the two checkpoints on Driving Score or on collisions. The Bench2Drive Success Rate was not detectably higher for `Balanced-6400` over both passes (+7.95 pp, CI [−2.27, 17.05]; Supplementary Section S4.4).

Is the balanced checkpoint faster? It is (post hoc, descriptive). The Driving Efficiency of Bench2Drive [14], the speed of the ego vehicle as a percentage of that of the surrounding traffic, was higher for `Balanced-6400` (paired difference of the two-pass means +22.89 points, CI [6.14, 34.89], on the 41 routes with a record in all four runs). The measure is taken at 20 route checkpoints per route (Table 2), and a record is written only when the vehicle passes one, so it leaves out a final stop and understates waiting; Table 6 gives the value of each pass, and Supplementary Section S4.6 the other measures of speed. Faster is not the same as better: the infraction penalty factor of `Balanced-6400`, of which a lower value is a larger penalty, was lower in the second pass and not detectably lower over both passes (Supplementary Section S4.6).

Do the two main differences depend on the routes? Not detectably (post hoc). On the 37 routes not touched by training samples and on the 24 routes not used to tune the controller, the differences in near-stationary plans and in standstill time had intervals below zero, over both passes and in each pass alone (Supplementary Section S4.4). One limit applies to all of this section: two passes address evaluation noise, of which a difference between server processes (Section 4.2) is a part, and they do not address variation between training runs, because each mix has one checkpoint.


## 4.4 The Matched Pair without the Standstill Gate

Protocol. Both 6,400-sample checkpoints were run once on the 44 routes with a controller that is identical to the default one except that the forced zero target speed and the holding brake under the gate condition are removed. The reading was fixed in advance (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §4c): a difference that persists without the gate supports an origin in the plans, and a difference that shrinks toward the noise reference, the difference between the two default passes, indicates that the gate amplifies it. The comparator with the gate is the per-route mean of the two default passes (Table 4). The analysis is exploratory, with one pass per checkpoint.

Does the difference in full stops, that is, in standstill time below 0.1 m/s, need the gate? Largely (Table 5, Figure 5). Without the gate `Balanced-6400` stood still for 18.76 % of the route time and `Hazard-heavy-6400` for 23.41 % (−4.65 pp, CI [−11.44, 3.20]); with the gate the difference was −23.54 pp. The signed-rank test rejects for the gate-off difference (p = 0.0048) while the interval of its mean includes zero, so a smaller difference in the same direction is not excluded. Removing the gate lowered the standstill of `Hazard-heavy-6400` by 24.14 pp and that of `Balanced-6400` by 5.24 pp (Table 5), a difference of +18.89 pp between the two changes (CI [11.29, 27.16]). The same quantity between the two default passes, with no change of controller, was +0.30 pp (CI [−8.75, 9.62]). By the reading fixed in advance, the gate amplifies the difference in full stops.

What persists without the gate? The difference in the plans and in slow driving. `Balanced-6400` returned a near-stationary plan in 0.60 % of steps and `Hazard-heavy-6400` in 14.58 % (−13.99 pp, CI [−20.27, −9.52]). The time below 1.0 m/s differed by −21.40 pp (CI [−28.67, −12.77]), because `Hazard-heavy-6400` now crawled where it had stood still. Blocked time stayed with `Balanced-6400` (12.62 % against 0.35 %; +12.27 pp, CI [5.14, 22.77]). By the same reading, these differences support an origin in the plans. The rate of near-stationary plans is not a property of the model alone. Without the gate `Hazard-heavy-6400`, with the same weights, returned such plans about half as often as with it (−13.46 pp, CI [−19.34, −8.65]). We read this as a feedback between gate and plans, which this run does not test directly (Supplementary Section S4.7).

What did removing the gate do to the outcomes? For `Hazard-heavy-6400` it exchanged waiting for progress and collisions at an unchanged score. ## 4.5 The Decoupling Checkpoint

Does the scenario mix or the manoeuvre mix carry the difference? `Decoupling-6400` has the scenario-group mix of the hazard-heavy set and the manoeuvre mix and stationary share of the balanced set (Section 3.3). Its readings, from one pass of the 44 routes, were fixed before the checkpoint had run any route (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §6 and §15) and are applied unchanged, with the per-route mean of the two passes of each of the other two checkpoints as comparator. Two readings were fixed. The plan-level reading compares the share of near-stationary plans of `Decoupling-6400` with that of each of the other two checkpoints: if it is lower than that of the hazard-heavy checkpoint with an interval below zero, and its difference from the balanced checkpoint has an interval that includes zero, near-stationary planning is read as going with the manoeuvre mix and the stationary share and not with the scenario-group mix. By the remedy reading, the mix may be called a candidate remedy only if it has fewer near-stationary plans than the hazard-heavy checkpoint, no more blocked time and no lower Driving Score, each judged by its interval. The rate of near-stationary plans of `Decoupling-6400` was not detectably different from that of the balanced checkpoint (Figure 3). `Decoupling-6400` returned a near-stationary plan in 1.58 % of steps, against 28.04 % for `Hazard-heavy-6400` (−26.47 pp, CI [−32.42, −20.84]) and 1.22 % for `Balanced-6400` (+0.36 pp, CI [−0.33, 1.74]). Standstill time gave the same reading (Supplementary Section S4.8).

How far does this reading go? First, the decoupling set shares 86.3 % of its samples with the balanced set (5,523 of 6,400), with the same seed, schedule and pool, so `Decoupling-6400` is not an independent repetition of `Balanced-6400`: it shows that replacing the other 877 samples did not bring the near-stationary plans back. The observed brake and hazard shares of the set remained those of the balanced set (Table 1), so what was varied is the scenario group from which the samples come, not how often they contain braking or a hazard. Second, the design fills three of four cells: no checkpoint has the balanced scenario-group mix with the other two factors of the hazard-heavy set, so the reading holds at the balanced manoeuvre mix and stationary share only.

Against `Balanced-6400` no measure differed detectably. The checkpoint has one training seed and one pass, and the two factors it shares with the balanced mix remain bundled (Supplementary Section S4.8).

## 4.6 The 1,600-Sample Pair

Does the difference between the mixes appear at 1,600 samples? In the plans it does, by a small margin. `Balanced-1600` and `Hazard-heavy-1600` were run once on the 44 routes. Three tests for this pair were fixed after `Balanced-1600` had finished and while `Hazard-heavy-1600` was running, before any value of either run was computed (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §31, where they are named S1 to S3). N1 (primary) and N2 apply the rules of F1 and F2 to this pair. N3 is the Driving Efficiency, met if the difference is positive, its interval lies above zero and p < 0.05; N2 and N3 are Holm-adjusted over the two. N1 was met: 4.92 % for `Balanced-1600` against 8.34 % for `Hazard-heavy-1600` (−3.42 pp, CI [−7.74, −0.11], p = 0.047). The interval ends close to zero and the p-value lies close to its threshold, so we report a difference that is present and small. It has the direction and about the size of the difference on identical replayed inputs (−2.75 pp; Supplementary Section S4.3), which was known when the tests were fixed.

Does it reach the motion of the vehicle? The evidence is as close to the border as for the plans. Standstill time was 24.93 % against 33.41 % (−8.49 pp, CI [−17.19, 0.46], Holm p = 0.030): the rank test rejects and the interval of the mean includes zero, which the rule of N2 reads as inconclusive. N1 passes its rule narrowly and N2 misses its rule narrowly, so the two verdicts are not a contrast between plans and motion. The Driving Efficiency did not differ detectably (97.83 against 102.30; −4.47 points, CI [−16.61, 2.29], p = 0.19; 43 routes; N3 is not met), and its sign is opposite to that of the 6,400-sample pair. Driving Score, route completion and collision events did not differ detectably (means in Table 6; intervals in Supplementary Section S4.9).

Is the difference smaller than at 6,400 samples? Yes, on all three tested measures: the difference of the 6,400-sample pair minus that of the 1,600-sample pair was −23.41 pp for near-stationary plans (CI [−31.39, −15.49]), −15.05 pp for standstill time (CI [−26.97, −4.16]) and +26.99 points for Driving Efficiency (CI [10.56, 40.03]; 41 routes). The stay-put shares of the training sets differ almost as much at 1,600 as at 6,400 samples (Table 1), so the training share alone does not account for this, and the design cannot separate the number of samples from the number of optimizer steps.

Where do the two checkpoints lie relative to the unadapted policy (descriptive)? Of the two, `Balanced-1600` returned fewer near-stationary plans than `Base-Rerun`; for `Hazard-heavy-1600` the difference was not detectable (Supplementary Section S4.9). Each 1,600-sample checkpoint has one training seed, an interrupted and resumed training run and one pass, so no pass-to-pass variation is available for this pair.

## 4.7 The Fine-Tuned Checkpoints against the Unadapted Policy (Post Hoc)

Did fine-tuning move the policy toward staying put or away from it? In both directions in these checkpoints. This comparison was added after the first pass was known and has no decision rule, and the unadapted policy has one pass (`Base-Rerun`, Section 4.1), so its own pass-to-pass variation is not averaged out. `Base-Rerun` returned a near-stationary plan in 13.24 % of steps. `Balanced-6400` lay below it (1.22 % over both passes; −12.02 pp, CI [−18.04, −7.75]) and `Hazard-heavy-6400` above it (28.04 %; +14.80 pp, CI [7.95, 21.62]), as on identical inputs (Section 4.2). Standstill time followed: −12.27 pp for `Balanced-6400` (CI [−21.55, −3.85]) and +11.27 pp for `Hazard-heavy-6400` (CI [1.00, 20.86]), against 36.28 % for `Base-Rerun`.

Did fine-tuning raise the benchmark score? For `Balanced-6400` the evidence is indicative. Its Driving Score over both passes was 51.52 against 42.60 (+8.92 points, CI [0.96, 18.04], p = 0.058): the interval excludes zero and the Wilcoxon test does not reject. For `Hazard-heavy-6400` and `Decoupling-6400` the interval of the difference includes zero (means in Table 6; intervals in Supplementary Section S4.10).


## 4.8 Motion-History Swap Test (Exploratory)

Protocol. The usual account of the inertia problem is that a stay-put plan follows the policy's own speed or recent motion and not the scene [6, 8, 10]. This exploratory test replaced the ego history of Section 3.1, the only non-visual motion input of the model, on about one step in five and read the plan that the model returned, without acting on it. On a step at 0.5 m/s or less the history was replaced by a *moving history*, a straight line behind the vehicle at a constant 4.0 m/s; the camera frames and the navigation command were unchanged. `Hazard-heavy-6400` and `Balanced-6400` were each run once on eight routes. The outcomes, their reading and the limits below were fixed before any run with a replaced history existed and were not changed afterwards (ANALYSIS_ADDENDUM §11, §11-B and §32); a preliminary evaluation with this test preceded the runs of this section.

Outcomes. A replaced history changes the extent $L$ mechanically (Section 3.1), so the outcomes are defined on the planned change of speed over the plan, which depends on the learned accelerations only. A plan holds speed if its planned speed changes by less than 0.5 m/s over the plan, accelerates if it gains 0.5 m/s or more, decelerates if it loses 0.5 m/s or more, and ends at rest if its final speed is below 0.5 m/s (Supplementary Section S4.11 gives the exact definitions). Among steps at 0.5 m/s or less, W-a (primary) is the share of plans that hold speed under the moving history minus that share under the true history, and W-b is the same difference for plans that end at rest. A checkpoint with fewer than five routes of at least 20 steps in both conditions receives no reading. By the rule, the learned controls *depend on the ego history* if the interval of W-a excludes zero. The stay-put plans may be tied to a history that reports rest only if, in addition, the share of plans that hold speed is lower under the moving history.

Do the controls of the hazard-heavy checkpoint depend on the ego history? They do, and not in the direction that this rule names. On the six routes that count, `Hazard-heavy-6400` planned to hold its speed in 84.29 % of the steps with the moving history and in 52.29 % of the steps with the true history (W-a: +32.00 pp, CI [22.63, 36.04], Wilcoxon p = 0.031, the smallest value possible with six routes). It planned to accelerate in 15.71 % against 47.65 %. The interval of W-a excludes zero, so the learned controls depend on the ego history. The share of plans that hold speed rose under the moving history and did not fall, so the condition for tying the stay-put plans to a history that reports rest is not met, and we report the outcome as found: told that it was moving at 4.0 m/s, this checkpoint more often planned to keep that speed and less often planned to accelerate.

Are the stay-put plans matched by braking plans? They are not. With the true history, 44.76 % of the plans of `Hazard-heavy-6400` at low speed ended at rest; with the moving history none did (W-b: −44.76 pp, CI [−50.51, −36.42], p = 0.031), and none decelerated. In this action space a plan that stays put at rest is a plan without acceleration, so this outcome is expected if the controls do not change, and by the rule it is not by itself evidence that the model uses the history.

What about the balanced checkpoint? There is no reading: `Balanced-6400` was rarely at low speed, and only three of its eight routes had 20 steps in both conditions. Supplementary Section S4.11 also gives the full protocol, a sensitivity check and descriptive results for a second replacement, a stationary history on steps where the vehicle was moving, which carry no reading.

Limits. The test replaced the ego-history tokens only; the camera window, which also shows whether the vehicle moves, was unchanged, so a visual route to the same behaviour was not tested. A replaced step measures the immediate plan, not how long a stop lasts. The test has eight routes, one pass, unseeded sampling and no reference model: `Balanced-6400` gave no reading and the unadapted policy was not tested. Three readings of W-a are not separated: a policy that uses its speed correctly would also plan to accelerate less often when its history reports 4.0 m/s; the class of plans that hold speed covers staying at rest under the true history and keeping 4.0 m/s under the moving one, which are different behaviours; and a moving history with camera frames that show no motion is an input the model was not trained on, so the outcome may be its response to an input it has not seen.



5. Discussion and Limitations

The two 6,400-sample checkpoints differed by −26.83 pp over both passes in how often they returned a near-stationary plan, and a difference in stay-put plans in the same direction was present on identical replayed inputs. With one checkpoint per mix we cannot separate the effect of the mix from that of the training run; a second pass addresses evaluation noise only. Two observations bear on this, and neither removes it. `Decoupling-6400`, trained in a separate run, returned near-stationary plans about as rarely as the balanced checkpoint, but its set shares 86.3 % of its samples with the balanced set, so this is closer to a second run on nearly the same data than to an independent test. The unadapted policy lay between the two checkpoints, which gives the direction of each relative to its starting point and does not bear on variation between training runs. Of the five fine-tuned checkpoints only `Hazard-heavy-6400` had a rate of near-stationary plans detectably above that of the unadapted policy in closed loop (Table 6). Each mix still has one seed, and the two remaining factors are bundled.

The behaviour matches the description of the inertia problem by Codevilla et al. [6]: a policy that, once slow, keeps predicting that it stays where it is. The match is behavioural. We do not show that the stops of `Hazard-heavy-6400` were unwarranted, and on the replayed states `Balanced-6400` stayed put less often than the demonstrations themselves, so the comparison ranks the checkpoints by how often they stay put and not by how well they drive. The behaviour is consistent with the conjecture of Section 3.2, which does not account for `Balanced-6400` planning to stay put far less often than its own training share, nor for the small difference at 1,600 samples; coarse sampling is a rival mechanism that we did not test. Since a plan that stays put at rest is a plan without acceleration, the difference can also be read as one in how often the model issues a start.

In the exploratory history-swap test the learned controls of `Hazard-heavy-6400` depended on its ego history, by the rule fixed in advance. This outcome is reported as found and does not identify why the checkpoint stays put (Section 4.8 gives the limits).

The controller decides how much of the difference in plans becomes a difference in standstill. Without the gate the difference in full stops shrank from −23.54 pp to −4.65 pp, with an interval that includes zero while the rank test still rejects, and the differences in near-stationary plans and in slow driving remained (Section 4.4). This supports an origin of the difference in the plans, with the gate amplifying its expression as full stops (exploratory, one pass). Because the gate may also feed back on the plans, the closed-loop rate of near-stationary plans describes the model together with its controller. Removing the gate was not a remedy: the hazard-heavy checkpoint then completed more of each route with more collision events and no detectable change in Driving Score, the pattern that Que et al. [40] report for a release rule on a different planner. Neither a held stop nor a blocked vehicle solves those scenarios.

Less standstill showed as higher driving efficiency (post hoc), not as a higher Driving Score. Over both passes the balanced checkpoint completed more of each route, its Driving Score was not detectably higher (+3.96 points), and its Driving Efficiency was higher by 22.89 points (Section 4.3). The last comparison is post hoc and descriptive: it was added after the Driving Score had shown no detectable difference, the measure does not count a final stop or blocked time as a cost, and at 1,600 samples the pre-specified test on it was not met, with a point estimate of the opposite sign. Collision events did not differ detectably over both passes, per route or per distance driven. We therefore describe the balanced 6,400-sample checkpoint as more efficient by this post hoc measure, at a Driving Score and a collision count that were not detectably different, not as driving better, and with three checkpoints we do not describe a trade-off curve. For the 6,400-sample pair the results are consistent with a trade-off in which the checkpoint that stands still less advances farther and spends more time commanded to move without moving, in most cases within 10 m of a recorded collision position (Section 4.3). Against the hazard-heavy checkpoint, `Decoupling-6400` showed the same pattern of fewer near-stationary plans, more route completion and more blocked time, with no detectable difference in Driving Score and with more collision events per route (Section 4.5). The pattern is specific to these contrasts: at 1,600 samples route completion did not differ detectably and blocked time was low in both checkpoints, and `Balanced-6400` stood still less than the unadapted policy without completing detectably more of each route (Table 6; Supplementary Sections S4.9 and S4.10). Whether a stop was required is not known from our logs, so less standstill cannot be read as better, and we do not recommend one mix over the other. For evaluation practice, standstill should be reported split by command and next to collisions. A several-fold difference in plans did not show in the Driving Score, and a comparison on that score needs repeated passes: a gain in the first pass did not recur in the second.

Limitations.

Design. Each condition is one checkpoint from one training run (seed 42), and the 1,600-sample runs were interrupted and resumed. The intervals describe route-to-route variation for these checkpoints, not variation between training runs. The mixes differ in group mix, lateral balance and stationary share together; the decoupling checkpoint separates only the scenario-group mix and has one seed and one pass. We studied one policy, one simulator and one controller.

Evaluation. Closed-loop runs are not deterministic: between the two passes of a checkpoint the per-route standstill differed with a standard deviation of 18 to 25 pp (Section 4.1). One server process served all routes of a job without clearing its streaming cache, and two processes of the same model did not return identical plans on all replayed states, for a reason we did not find, so a difference between two jobs includes a difference between two server processes. The second pass and the same-input replay address this for the 6,400-sample pair; the results with one pass (the decoupling checkpoint, the runs without the gate, the 1,600-sample pair and the unadapted policy) remain exposed to it, and their contrasts in Driving Score and collision events are descriptive. The 44 routes are a subset of the benchmark, seven are touched by training samples, and the controller was tuned on 20 of them with a fine-tuned checkpoint, which can favour fine-tuned models over the unadapted one. The gate-off controller was written after first results of these checkpoints were known. The 60 s standstill rule censors the standstill fraction. With this subset, our controller, Low rendering quality and CARLA 0.9.15, the Driving Scores and the Driving Efficiency are not comparable with published 220-route results.

Measurement and transfer. Blocked time is inferred from the command log, not from a contact sensor, and the bridge logs neither signal state nor actor distances, so we cannot say whether a stop was required. Scripted CARLA traffic does not react to a waiting or blocked ego vehicle as human drivers would, so the costs that either behaviour imposes on others are not represented. Inference uses a VLM quantized to 4-bit weights and 8-bit activations with streaming cache reuse, whereas training uses full-context BF16. The training data were collected at rendering quality Epic (taken from the collection code; we have no log of the collection run) and evaluation ran at Low, a visual domain shift that may affect all fine-tuned models and could moderate the differences we report. We did not compare against controller-side anti-stall rules or model-side remedies such as history dropout [10] or speed prediction [6].

Pre-specification. The tests were fixed after preliminary evaluations of the same checkpoints on the same routes (Section 4.1). They are pre-specified tests on new runs, not tests of hypotheses formed without sight of related data, and the route set was not held out.

Future work follows from these limits: further training seeds, a direct measurement of the stay-put sampling rate, the remaining routes, logging of contact and hazards, and comparisons with history dropout and a creep-style controller.


6. Conclusion, Availability and Disclosures

We compared two fine-tuning mixes, drawn from the same verified pool, for the flow-matching action expert of Alpamayo 1.5, with one training run per mix and dataset size. The hazard-heavy 6,400-sample checkpoint returned a near-stationary plan in 26.80 % of steps and the balanced one in 1.31 %. A test fixed before the runs was met (−25.49 pp, CI [−31.69, −19.63]), a repeat test on a second pass was met (−28.16 pp), and a difference in stay-put plans in the same direction was present on identical replayed inputs, where the unadapted policy lay between the two checkpoints, as it did in closed loop (Sections 4.2, 4.3 and 4.7). This behaviour matches the description of the inertia problem; we do not show that the stops were unwarranted. A third checkpoint, with the scenario-group shares of the hazard-heavy set and the manoeuvre mix and stationary share of the balanced set, returned near-stationary plans about as rarely as the balanced checkpoint (1.58 % of steps; three of the four cells of this design were run), so in these three training runs the rate went with the manoeuvre mix and the stationary share. On the road the balanced checkpoint stood still about half as long, completed more of each route and drove at a higher speed relative to the surrounding traffic (Driving Efficiency +22.89 points; post hoc, and the test of this measure at 1,600 samples was not met). It spent more time commanded to accelerate without moving and did not obtain a detectably higher Driving Score (+3.96 points over both passes); a gain seen in the first pass did not recur in the second, and collision events did not differ detectably per route (+0.24) or per distance driven (Section 4.3). Without the controller's standstill gate the difference in full stops shrank to −4.65 pp, with an interval that includes zero while the rank test still rejects, and the difference in near-stationary plans remained (−13.99 pp; Section 4.4), which supports an origin of the difference in the plans, with the gate turning short plans into held stops (exploratory, one pass). By the rule fixed in advance the checkpoint trained on the decoupling mix had fewer near-stationary plans at a cost (one training run), and the mix is not called a remedy. At 1,600 samples the differences were smaller and at the border of detection: −3.42 pp in near-stationary plans and −8.49 pp in standstill time (Section 4.6). In an exploratory test on six routes the learned controls of the hazard-heavy checkpoint depended on its ego-motion history (plans that hold speed: +32.00 pp under a history that reports motion; Section 4.8). The test does not identify the mechanism: the pre-specified condition for tying the stay-put plans to a history that reports rest was not met, correct use of speed would give the same outcome, and the camera input was not varied. For evaluation practice, standstill time should be reported split by command, and a comparison on Driving Score needs repeated passes. With one checkpoint per mix the dependence on composition across training seeds is untested. We make no safety claim.
